Color in plastic parts: masterbatch, color matching and tolerance

How a part gets its color, what masterbatch is, how a color is approved with Delta E and what to ask for so it stays consistent between lots.

7 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

The most common way to color a part is masterbatch: a pigment concentrate in pellet form that is mixed with the base resin at the press, usually at 1 to 5% by weight. Color is approved by comparing the part against a master sample with an instrument that reports the difference as Delta E. To keep color from drifting between lots, agree in writing on three things: the master sample, the Delta E tolerance and how each lot is checked.

How does a plastic part get its color?

There are three main routes. The choice depends on volume, how demanding the color is and the dosing equipment on the press.

What is masterbatch?

It is a concentrated mix of pigments or additives, already melted and turned into pellets with a carrier material. The active content is usually 40 to 65% of the masterbatch. The carrier is a polymer identical or compatible with the base resin, for example a polyolefin for PP and PE, or polystyrene for ABS.

It lets you dose a small amount of pigment accurately, avoids dust and lets you combine several colors or additives in one process. Masterbatch is not only for color: there are also grades for UV protection or flame retardancy. To place masterbatch among the other raw materials, see the guide to raw materials for injection molding.

Illustrative dosing example

With a 2% masterbatch (one part concentrate to 50 parts resin), a 50 g part carries 1 g of masterbatch and 49 g of natural resin. A 1,000 kg resin lot needs 20 kg of masterbatch.

How is color measured and approved?

Human eyes vary from person to person and with the light. So color is measured with a spectrophotometer, which expresses it in three values of the CIELAB space: L* (lightness), a* (red to green) and b* (yellow to blue). The distance between the reference color and the part color is the Delta E (ΔE).

There are several formulas. CIE76 is the simplest and oldest; in it a ΔE of about 2.3 matches the smallest difference the eye notices. CIEDE2000 corrects flaws in the earlier ones and is the one usually requested today. According to Formlabs, a ΔE 2000 below 1 is not perceivable by the human eye.

What makes color change between lots?

  • Masterbatch or resin lot: two lots of the same product can differ slightly. Ask for a color certificate per lot.
  • Dosing: a small drift changes strength.
  • Temperature and residence time: some pigments degrade with heat or a long stay in the barrel.
  • Part thickness and texture: a thin wall looks lighter than a thick one, and a texture changes how the color reads.
  • Recycled material: the base color varies and makes matching harder. We cover it in the guide to recycled plastic in injection molding.
  • Lighting: two colors that match under one light can look different under another. Ask for the measurement under the illuminant your end customer uses.

Common mistake

Ordering a color by name or by a catalog reference only. Catalogs look different on paper, on a screen and on plastic. Provide a physical sample, the measured L*a*b* values and the Delta E tolerance. With that, any lot is compared against a numeric criterion and not an opinion.

What should you ask for to keep color stable?

  • A physical master sample approved by both sides, kept by the molder and by you.
  • L*a*b* values of the reference, the ΔE formula (for example CIEDE2000), the illuminant and the maximum tolerance.
  • A single masterbatch supplier, with a color certificate per lot.
  • A color measurement on the first part of every lot and a record of the result.
  • Written notice before any change of resin, masterbatch or supplier.

Color is approved together with the rest of the first sample, as described in the guide to PPAP and first article inspection. To understand how the base resin affects the result, see the guide to resin selection.
