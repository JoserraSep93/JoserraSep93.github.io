What you need to get a plastic part quoted

The exact list of files and data that speeds up a quote, and what happens when something is missing.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A firm quote needs two files and six pieces of information: the 3D model in STEP or native format, the drawing with tolerances, the resin or end-use conditions, annual volume, the tooling situation, secondary operations, packaging with destination, and the target date. With the 3D model, the material and a volume range, a molder can already give you a budgetary price.

An injection molding quote comes from three calculations: what mold is needed, which press runs the part, and how long each cycle takes. Every piece of information you send feeds one of those calculations. When something is missing, the estimator assumes, and you pay for that assumption later in price revisions, tool changes or lost weeks.

This guide goes deeper than our how to quote page: which file format to send, what each data point is used for, and exactly what happens when you don't have it.

What files do you need to quote an injection molded part?

Two: a solid 3D model and a 2D drawing. The model describes the geometry. The drawing tells the molder which dimensions matter, to what tolerance and with what finish. One does not replace the other.

The safest 3D format is STEP (.stp or .step), a neutral standard (ISO 10303) that any mold design software can open. The most widely used US quoting platforms accept STEP, IGES, Parasolid and native files; Protolabs Network does not accept STL or OBJ for injection molding because those formats lack the precision a mold requires.

The most common mistake: sending only an STL

An STL approximates the part with triangles. It is fine for viewing, but it cannot give a toolmaker exact radii or true flat faces to machine in steel. Converting an STL to STEP does not bring the lost precision back: export from the original CAD file.

If you have no file at all, a physical sample or photos with measurements are enough to start. The part gets modeled first, and you approve the model before the mold is quoted. To check your file before sending it, you can view your part in 3D.

Why does annual volume change the mold and the price?

Because volume sets how many cycles the mold must survive. The SPI classification, from the US plastics industry association, groups molds by expected life. Each class uses different steels, hardness and construction, and therefore carries a different price.

Example: you need 200,000 parts a year for 5 years. With a single-cavity mold that is 1,000,000 cycles, which calls for a Class 101 tool. With 4 cavities, 250,000 cycles are enough, but both the mold and the press get bigger.

5 yearsprogram life
1,000,000cycles with 1 cavity
250,000cycles with 4 cavities

That trade-off is worked through step by step in how many cavities your mold should have, and its effect on price in how much an injection mold costs. If you don't know the exact volume, a range is enough: "50,000 to 100,000 a year" already lets the molder choose the mold class.

How does part size affect the press you need?

The press has to hold the mold shut against the pressure of the melt. That clamp force is estimated by multiplying the part's projected area, its shadow seen straight into the mold, by a factor that depends on the resin.

Example: a 300 × 200 mm (about 11.8 × 7.9 in) lid has about 93 square inches of projected area. Using Rosti's factors (2.5 to 3.5 tons per square inch for PP, HDPE and ABS; 4 to 5 for polycarbonate), the same lid needs a different press depending on the resin.

That is why the 3D model is essential: it gives the area, the weight and the mold size, and the mold also has to fit between the press tie bars. SDH runs presses from 125 to 1,300 tons; see the details on our capabilities page.

What finish and tolerances should you specify?

Finish is specified on the SPI scale, from A-1 (mirror) to D-3 (coarse blast), or with a texture supplier's code. Each grade is a different mold polishing process with a different cost.

Texture also changes the design. The common rule is to add 1.5° of draft for every 0.001 in (0.025 mm) of texture depth. A part designed with 0.5° of draft and then ordered with a coarse texture can drag or stick on ejection. That gets caught in a design for manufacturing review.

For tolerances, call out only the dimensions that affect function: assemblies, seals, threads, mating features. Plastic shrinks as it cools (PP between 1.0% and 3.0%, ABS between 0.4% and 0.8%, per the Rosti chart), so applying machined-metal tolerances to the whole drawing drives up mold and inspection cost. ISO 20457 sets tolerances specifically for molded plastic parts because metal rules cannot be applied directly.

What happens after you send the request?

Can you get a quote without all the information?

Yes. With the 3D model, the material or end-use conditions and a volume range, you get a budgetary price good enough to make decisions. The key is knowing which assumptions sit behind that price, because every item that arrives later can move it. Ask for the assumptions in writing and check them against this list.

How SDH does it

We sign an NDA before receiving your drawings. We take STEP, IGES, Parasolid or native files; if all you have is a sample, we model it with you.
