Raw materials for injection molding: resins, additives and preparation

Resin types, additives, drying and how to read a datasheet, so you know which material to use and how it affects price per part.

8 min read · October 22, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

The raw material for injection molding is resin in pellet form, sometimes blended with color, additives or fiber. It falls into three tiers: commodity plastics (PP, PE, PS), engineering plastics (ABS, PC, PA, POM, PET) and high-performance plastics. Each resin has its own shrinkage, processing temperature and, in several cases, a drying requirement before it goes into the machine. Choosing well sets the price per part, and preparing it well prevents defects.

When someone searches for injection molding raw materials, they usually want three things: what the options are, how they differ and what to ask the supplier. This guide is the big picture. To decide between two or three specific resins, go to the guide on how to choose the resin.

What exactly is the raw material in injection molding?

Pellets: plastic grains 2 to 4 mm across, like thick rice, delivered in bags, big bags or bulk. The machine heats them until they melt, pushes the melt into the mold and cools it until the part solidifies. Pellets can be uncolored virgin resin, pre-colored resin, a compound with additives or fiber, or ground recycled material.

What types of plastic are used in injection molding?

They are grouped by performance and price. Commodity plastics are cheap and used in volume. Engineering plastics cost more and handle more heat, load or wear. High-performance plastics (PEEK, PPS, PEI) serve very specific cases and are left out of this table. The values are typical industry ranges; the exact grade changes with each producer.

What is the difference between amorphous and semi-crystalline resin?

Picture a plate of spaghetti. In an amorphous resin (ABS, PC, PS, PMMA) the chains stay tangled as they cool. They soften gradually, shrink little and are often transparent or give a good finish. In a semi-crystalline resin (PP, PE, PA, POM) part of the chains line up in order, like spaghetti packed in the box. Those ordered zones take less space, so the part shrinks more, looks more opaque and resists chemicals and wear better.

The practical consequence: a mold is sized for one resin. If you switch resins later, dimensions can drift out of tolerance. More on this in the guide on tolerances in injection molded parts.

What additives go into the resin?

Almost no part is molded from pure resin. The most common additives are:

  • Colorants and masterbatch. Masterbatch is a pigment concentrate in pellet form that is dosed at the machine, usually 1 to 4% by weight. It is cleaner and more flexible than buying pre-colored resin.
  • UV stabilizers. They keep sunlight from fading and embrittling the part. Essential outdoors and in the field.
  • Flame retardants. They make the part resist fire or self-extinguish. Used in electrical and electronic housings; they cost some toughness.
  • Glass fiber. Raises stiffness and lowers shrinkage, in exchange for more mold wear and a less smooth finish. See the guide on glass-filled plastics.
  • Impact modifiers. They add toughness to resins that turn brittle in the cold or under impact.

Can recycled or reground resin be used?

Yes, with conditions. Regrind is the runners, flash and rejected parts that get ground up and blended with virgin resin at a controlled percentage. Post-consumer recycled resin is a separate category, with more lot-to-lot variation. In both cases you should set a maximum percentage, measure the flow index of each lot and get customer approval. Percentages, colors and testing are covered in the guide on recycled plastic in injection molding.

Why do some resins need to be dried?

Because some absorb moisture from the air. They are called hygroscopic: PA, PC, PET and, to a lesser degree, ABS. If they enter the machine wet, the water turns to steam at processing temperature and breaks the polymer chains. The result is silver streaks, bubbles, brittle parts or lower strength, even when the part looks almost normal. PP and HDPE absorb almost no water and normally are not dried.

How do you read a resin datasheet (TDS)?

A datasheet has dozens of lines. For most projects, three are enough:

  • Melt flow index (MFI or MFR). It measures how many grams of resin flow through a standard orifice in 10 minutes, at a set temperature and weight. A high value flows easily and suits thin walls or long flow paths. A low value is more viscous and usually gives more mechanical strength. Only compare values measured under the same conditions.
  • Density. It sets how many grams each cubic centimeter of part weighs, and so how much material you buy per unit.
  • Shrinkage. It almost always appears as a range. It is the number the mold designer needs to compensate dimensions.

Datasheet values are typical, not guaranteed, and are measured on lab test bars. Use them to compare, then validate with real samples.

How does the material change the price per part?

Material affects price in three ways: the resin price per pound, density (higher density means more grams for the same volume) and its effect on the process, such as longer cycles or drying. A resin that is cheaper per pound can cost more per part if it is denser or needs more machine time. Each resin's price also moves on its own in the market, so quote with the current figure. To see how the full cost is built, read the guide on price per part in injection molding. To compare the two most used polyolefins, see PP vs HDPE.

Common mistake

Choosing the resin on price per pound alone. An engineering resin may allow thinner walls, fewer rejected parts or replace a metal piece in the assembly, and end up cheaper per finished part.
