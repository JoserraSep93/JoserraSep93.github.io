Minimum volume for injection molding: when it pays off and when it does not

How many parts it takes for injection molding to beat 3D printing, machining or thermoforming, with the crossover point.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Injection molding starts to win somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand parts, depending on part size and mold cost. Below that, 3D printing or machining usually costs less. The exact number comes from a simple calculation: mold cost divided by the per-part cost difference between the two processes.

What is the minimum volume for injection molding to make sense?

There is no technical minimum: a mold can make 10 parts. The minimum is economic. Injection molding has a high fixed cost (the mold) and a very low cost per part. 3D printing and machining have almost no fixed cost, but every part costs much more.

1 to 50parts: typical 3D printing range according to Protolabs
1 to 200parts: typical CNC machining range
200+parts: where injection molding comes in

These are reference bands, not rules. Xometry places the breakeven between 3D printing and injection molding between 250 and 2,000 parts. Protolabs notes that at a thousand parts or fewer, machining plastic can cost less than molding it.

Why is injection molding expensive at first and cheap later?

Because the mold is paid for once, and after that each part only carries resin and a few seconds of press time. In the Formlabs example, the mold cost USD 3,600 and each molded part USD 0.32. The printed part cost about USD 0.60, with no mold. What decides it is how many parts you need for the per-part savings to pay off the mold.

Where is the crossover point against 3D printing and machining?

Assumptions (illustrative example of a mid-size housing, not a quote):

  • 3D printing (sintered nylon): USD 22 per part, no fixed cost.
  • CNC machining: USD 500 for programming and setup, plus USD 15 per part.
  • Injection molding with an aluminum mold: USD 5,000 mold, plus USD 1.20 per part.
  • Injection molding with a steel mold: USD 25,000 mold, plus USD 0.60 per part.

How to read it: at 1,000 parts, printing costs USD 22,000 in total, machining 15,500 and injection molding 6,200 including the mold. The molding line starts high but barely climbs.

How do I calculate my own crossover point?

You need two numbers for each process: the fixed cost (mold, programming or setup) and the variable cost per part. The formula is:

Crossover parts = (fixed cost of process B − fixed cost of process A) / (cost per part of A − cost per part of B)

Using the example, machining vs. molding with an aluminum mold: (5,000 − 500) / (15 − 1.20) = 4,500 / 13.80 = 326 parts. Below that number, machine; above it, mold. Ask each supplier for both numbers separately and run the math yourself.

What shifts the crossover point?

  • Part size: on large parts, printing or machining costs a lot per part, so molding wins sooner. In the Xometry example, a 216 mm junction housing printed in nylon cost over USD 200, and molding was already cheaper from about 250 parts.
  • Small parts: the opposite. In the Formlabs example, a small mixer latch printed in-house broke even with molding only at 13,050 parts.
  • Mold type: an aluminum mold lowers the fixed cost and moves the crossover earlier. We compare both in aluminum vs. steel molds.
  • Part properties: if you need the production resin (PP, HDPE, ABS) for testing or certification, 3D printing does not offer all of them, and going to a mold earlier makes sense.
Common mistake

Looking only at the first order. If you need 300 parts today but the product will sell 5,000 a year, run the numbers on the product's lifetime volume. In the example, 5,000 machined parts cost USD 75,500; molded with an aluminum mold, USD 11,000.

What about thermoforming?

Thermoforming tools cost less than injection molds, but the process only fits open, thin-walled parts such as trays or covers. It cannot make ribs, threads or varying wall thickness. If your part has that shape, compare all four processes in injection, blow molding, thermoforming or rotomolding.

Is there a minimum order per production run?

Beyond the mold, every run has a startup cost: hanging the mold, heating up, purging the previous resin and adjusting until good parts come out. That cost is spread over the parts in the lot.

That is why many molders set a minimum lot. If your usage is low but steady, one option is to run larger lots and hold them in stock. We break down the full price per part in how the price per part is calculated.
