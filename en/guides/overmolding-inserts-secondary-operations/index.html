Overmolding, metal inserts and secondary operations: when they make sense

What is integrated in the mold and what happens after molding, what it adds to cost and which assembly it saves.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Build into the mold what repeats on every part and will not change: a metal insert, a rubber grip, a second layer. Leave for after molding what changes by customer or order: labels, kits, assemblies with purchased parts. Decide on joint performance and cost per part, weighing the seconds you add to the cycle against the manual operation you remove.

Which operations happen in the mold and which happen after molding?

There are two families. Those that happen inside the mold during injection: overmolding, insert molding and in-mold labeling. And those that happen afterward: installing components in the cooled part, welding, labeling and assembly.

When does overmolding make sense?

When a part needs two properties one plastic cannot deliver: stiffness and grip, or stiffness and a seal. There are two ways to do it. With pick-and-place, the substrate is molded, moved to a second mold and overmolded. With two-shot molding, a special press does it in a single cycle.

Protolabs puts the crossover at 10,000 parts: below it, pick-and-place tends to cost less in total; above it, two-shot wins. Overmolding also removes the recurring cost of assembling thousands of parts.

The bond between materials can be chemical, if they are compatible, or mechanical, through holes, grooves or undercuts in the substrate. Protolabs recommends a mechanical lock when the bond is critical and avoiding exposed edges of the overmold, since that is where peeling starts.

Metal insert in the mold or after molding?

A molded-in insert is fully surrounded by plastic and gives the best pull-out and torque resistance. In exchange, someone or something must load it every cycle. Post-mold methods work on the cooled part. Plastics Technology compared them this way:

Example: what an insert adds to cost per part

Assumptions (illustrative, not a quote): housing with 4 threaded inserts, 40 s cycle, press at $50 per hour. Heat staking station at $20 per hour including operator and equipment. Each heat-staked insert takes 5 s; loading 4 inserts into the mold adds 8 s to the cycle.

The lesson: if the operator has idle time within the cycle, a press-side secondary operation costs almost nothing. If the thread is critical or assembled many times, the molded-in insert is worth the extra cents. The full price math is in how the price per part is calculated.

When does welding or labeling in the plant make sense?

Ultrasonic welding joins two plastic parts in 0.5 to 2 s, at 20 to 70 kHz, according to Fictiv. It works best on amorphous plastics such as ABS and PC; PP and nylon need more energy and a better joint design. The joint is permanent.

For labeling, in-mold labeling fuses the label to the part and removes the later step; it is used mostly on high-volume containers and needs a robot and special label stock. A label applied after molding lets you change design, language or code per order without touching the mold.

Common mistake

Designing the part first and thinking about the insert or overmold at the end. The insert hole, the overmold lock and the weld joint are designed together with the part. Adding them after the mold is built costs steel, time and sometimes a new mold. See the injection molding design rules and the design for manufacturing review.

How do I decide what to integrate?

Every operation done at the press also saves a material move and a work-in-process inventory. If the product ships assembled, define the export packaging from the start.
