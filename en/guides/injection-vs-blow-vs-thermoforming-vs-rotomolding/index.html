Injection, blow molding, thermoforming or rotomolding: which process fits your part

The four processes side by side: geometry, volume, tooling cost, tolerances and example parts.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Let geometry decide first and volume second. An open or solid part with ribs, snap fits or threads at high volume: injection molding. A hollow part with a neck, such as a bottle or jug: blow molding. A large open shell with detail on one side only: thermoforming. A very large hollow part with thick walls and low annual volume: rotomolding.

All four processes turn resin into a part, but each starts from a different form of plastic and uses a different kind of mold. That changes which shapes are possible, what the tooling costs, how many parts come off per hour and how tightly dimensions can be held.

What is the difference between injection, blow molding, thermoforming and rotomolding?

Injection molding: molten plastic is forced under pressure into a closed two-half mold. The part copies the mold on both sides, so it can carry ribs, bosses, snap fits and holes.

Extrusion blow molding: a hot tube of plastic (the parison) is extruded, the mold pinches it shut and air inflates it against the cavity walls. It makes hollow bodies with an opening: bottles, jugs, drums and tanks.

Thermoforming: a heated sheet is pulled over or into a mold with vacuum or pressure. It makes open shells: trays, covers, tubs and packaging.

Rotomolding: resin powder goes inside a mold that spins on two axes in an oven. The plastic melts and coats the walls with no pressure. It makes large, thick-walled hollow parts such as tanks and kayaks.

Which process has the cheapest tooling?

The one that runs at the lowest pressure. Machine Design ranks tooling cost from lowest to highest as vacuum forming, rotomolding, blow molding and injection molding. Injection pushes plastic in with a lot of force, which is why it needs steel, cooling channels and ejectors.

A steel production injection mold typically lands between $5,000 and more than $100,000, depending on size and complexity; the breakdown is in how much an injection mold costs. At the other end, the Association of Rotational Molders (ARM) puts blow mold amortization at 1.5 to 2.5 times that of rotomolding for a part that could be made either way.

Common mistake

Choosing the process by tooling price. At high volume, a 30-minute cycle or the trim scrap on every thermoformed sheet weighs more in cost per part than the upfront savings. Another frequent mistake is asking for thermoforming on a part designed with ribs, holes and snap fits: most of those features end up as secondary machining or assembly.

How many parts per hour does each process make?

This is where cost per part really separates. Xometry gives an example for a mid-size hollow part: about 70 parts per hour by blow molding versus about 2 by rotomolding. In injection molding, output depends on cycle time and the number of cavities.

What wall thickness and tolerance can you expect?

In injection molding the wall is what you drew, because the mold defines both faces. Protolabs works with ±0.003 in (0.08 mm) machining tolerance on the tool, plus a resin tolerance of at least 0.002 in/in. In the other three processes the wall results from how much the material stretches, which opens up the tolerance.

In thermoforming the sheet thins out where it stretches most, especially in deep corners. Blow molding behaves the same way: corners come out thinner and flat panels thicker. For rotomolding, Machine Design calls ±2% a wide tolerance and ±0.5% a tight one. If your part has critical dimensions, also read which tolerances injection molding can hold.

How do I pick the right process for my part in four questions?

If the answer lands between two processes, compare total cost per part with tooling spread over program volume. The math is in how price per part is calculated, and the crossover point is in minimum volume for injection molding.

Can one product combine several processes?

It is the norm. A blow-molded jug carries an injection-molded cap. A rotomolded tank can carry injection-molded fittings. A thermoformed cover can carry injection-molded brackets screwed on the inside. Designing each component for its own process usually costs less than forcing everything into one.

For containers, see how extrusion blow molding works. For molded parts, the injection molding design rules give you wall, draft and rib values.
