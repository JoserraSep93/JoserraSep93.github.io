How much an injection mold costs and what drives the price

The six factors that move a mold's price, with real ranges and how to lower the cost without compromising the part.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

An injection mold can cost anywhere from a few hundred dollars (a 3D-printed prototype tool) to more than $100,000 (steel, multiple cavities, hot runner). For a typical production part in steel, most projects land between $5,000 and $100,000. Six things drive the price: size, complexity, cavities, steel, finish and runner system.

How much does an injection mold cost?

It mostly depends on how many parts the mold must produce over its life and how complex the part is. A tool for 500 trial parts and a tool for a million parts a year are different products, with different steels, build times and prices.

One example from the same source: for a small enclosure, an aluminum mold for 5,000 parts ran about $3,000, and a steel mold for 100,000 parts about $20,000. Same part; what changed was the volume the tool had to survive.

What drives the price of a mold? The six factors

1. Part size

A large part needs a bigger steel block, more machining hours and a higher-tonnage press. A bucket or a tote sits at the top of the range; a small cap sits at the bottom.

2. Geometry complexity

Every undercut (a snap hook, a side hole, a thread) forces moving mechanisms inside the mold: slides, lifters or unscrewing cores. Each one adds steel, machining and fitting.

3. Number of cavities

A 4-cavity mold costs more than a single-cavity one, but not four times more: the mold base, ejection system and design are shared. In exchange, cost per part drops. We run the numbers in how many cavities your mold should have.

4. Steel and tool life

Aluminum is cheaper and machines fast, but it wears sooner. Hardened steel lasts hundreds of thousands or millions of cycles. Abrasive resins, such as glass-filled grades, call for harder steel.

5. Finish and tolerances

A mirror polish or special texture requires hand work. Tight tolerances mean more fitting and more trial rounds. Protolabs recommends polishing only the areas that need it.

6. Cold runner or hot runner

A hot runner keeps the plastic molten all the way to the part: no runner to scrap or regrind, and the cycle usually gets shorter. It raises the cost of the mold and its maintenance, so it pays off at high volumes.

Which mold class do you need?

The SPI classification (Society of the Plastics Industry) is the common language between buyer and toolmaker for tool life. Specifying the right class keeps you from paying for a million-cycle mold on a product that will sell 50,000 units.

Where does the money in a mold go?

A toolmaker in Asia published how its mold price splits. It helps explain why two quotes for the same part can be far apart: machining hours and steel change.

How much of each part price is the mold?

You pay for the mold once and spread it across every part it makes. That is why its share of unit cost falls as volume rises.

Should you build the mold in Mexico or in Asia?

Toolmakers in China usually quote lower. Pexco notes that an Asia-built mold can add around 25% to overall lead time, from shipping, communication and correction loops. The right comparison is total cost: price, freight, import, waiting time and how easy it is to fix the tool.

What should a mold quote include?

To compare toolmakers on equal terms, ask every quote to state:

  • Number of cavities and runner type (cold or hot).
  • Cavity and core steel, plus SPI class or expected life in cycles.
  • Number of slides, lifters and other mechanisms.
  • Finish by area and texture, if any.
  • How many sampling and correction rounds the price includes.
  • Press tonnage the mold is designed for.
  • Who owns the mold and its drawings.
