Mold flow simulation: what it is and when it is worth it

What a mold flow simulation predicts, which decisions it supports before cutting steel and in which projects it pays for itself.

6 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A mold flow simulation is a computer calculation that shows how molten plastic fills the cavity before the mold exists. It helps you place gates and anticipate weld lines, air traps and hard-to-fill areas. It pays off when the mold is expensive to fix: large parts, thin walls, multi-cavity tools or difficult resins. Its results are a prediction that the first samples confirm.

What is a mold flow simulation?

It is a digital model of the part, the resin and the process. From the 3D geometry, the material and injection data (temperature, fill time, pressure), the software calculates how the plastic front advances inside the cavity. The output is a set of color maps: fill time, pressure, temperature and the points where flows meet.

Some programs also calculate packing, cooling and how much the part warps after ejection. To see which mold components are being analyzed, read the guide on injection mold parts and types.

When is it worth it, and when is it not?

Simulation costs engineering time. It makes sense when a mistake in steel is expensive. For simple, thick-walled, single-cavity parts, an experienced designer is usually enough.

One reason to run it is the cost of fixing things late. A technical article in Digital Engineering 24/7 says a production problem can delay a launch by six to ten weeks, and that repairing a mold already in service can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Those figures come from a simulation provider, so treat them as an order of magnitude.

Illustrative example: a long, thin-walled part

Picture a PP lid 300 mm long with a 1.5 mm wall. The ratio of flow length to wall thickness is 200 to 1, which is high for that wall. Reference values depend on the resin grade, so this example is illustrative.

Moving a gate in the model takes hours. In a mold that is already machined it can mean welding, re-machining and testing again. That difference is what the simulation pays for.

What are its limits?

  • It depends on the inputs: if the resin in the model does not match the grade you will run, the result drifts.
  • It is better for comparing options than for exact prediction: weld line and air trap locations are taken as qualitative guidance.
  • It does not replace testing: the mold is validated with samples, as explained in the guide on PPAP and first article inspection.
  • It needs good geometry: an incomplete 3D file or wrongly modeled wall thicknesses give doubtful results.
Ask for the report, not just the conclusion

If a molder says they simulated your part, ask for the fill maps, the resin used in the model and the decisions that changed. A report without those details does not tell you what was checked.

How does it connect with part and mold design?

Simulation works best when the design already follows good practice. Uniform walls, radii and well-sized ribs reduce problems before you simulate: we cover them in the injection molding design rules. The results are also discussed in the design for manufacturing review, where you decide which changes to make before ordering the mold. If the analysis flags likely defects, the guide on injection molding defects explains their causes.
