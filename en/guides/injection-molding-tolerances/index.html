Injection molding tolerances: what is achievable and what drives cost

Typical tolerances by resin and size, why plastic shrinks and how to specify only what matters.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

With commercial molding, a 21 to 100 mm dimension (about 0.8 to 4 in) typically holds about ±0.15 mm in ABS and ±0.17 mm in PP or HDPE, per the industry reference table (SPI). Fine tolerance brings that down to ±0.10 or ±0.11 mm, with a costlier mold and tighter process control. Call out tight tolerances only on dimensions that assemble or seal.

What tolerance can injection molding hold?

A molded part's tolerance adds up two things: how precisely the mold was machined, and how much the resin varies as it shrinks. Quick-turn molders publish their general rules, and they make a good starting point.

±0.003 inmold machining tolerance published by Protolabs (±0.076 mm)
+0.002 in/inminimum added resin tolerance per inch of dimension
±0.004 inpart-to-part repeatability published by Xometry (±0.10 mm)

The rule is simple: tolerance = mold machining + 0.002 per unit of dimension length. Xometry uses the same logic with ±0.005 in machining. Both note it is a general tolerance and that tighter values are possible with more tool work.

What tolerance does each resin hold by size?

The reference table published by the former Society of the Plastics Industry (now the Plastics Industry Association) separates two grades. Commercial is what a standard mold and process deliver. Fine is what tighter control delivers, at a higher cost.

Flatness shows an even bigger gap. On a surface up to 100 mm, the same table gives 0.85 mm commercial flatness for PP, 0.38 mm for ABS and 0.15 mm for 30% glass-filled nylon. On a large flat part, the resin matters more than the mold. The guides on choosing a resin and glass-filled plastics go deeper on that choice.

Why does plastic shrink, and by how much?

Plastic enters the mold molten and shrinks as it cools. Amorphous resins such as ABS and polycarbonate shrink little and evenly. Semi-crystalline resins such as PP, HDPE, nylon and acetal form crystals as they cool: they shrink more, and shrinkage varies more with wall thickness, pressure and mold temperature.

The datasheet is what counts. Covestro, for example, reports 0.65% shrinkage in the flow direction and 0.70% across flow for its Makrolon 2405 polycarbonate, measured per ISO 294-4 on 2 mm plaques. That difference between directions is one of the causes of warpage.

Example: how much shrinkage moves a 100 mm dimension in PP

Assumptions (illustrative): a PP part with a 100 mm nominal dimension. The mold is cut for an expected average shrinkage of 1.75%, so the cavity measures 101.78 mm. Actual shrinkage may land anywhere between 1.5% and 2.0% depending on wall thickness and process.

  • At 1.5% shrinkage, the part measures 100.25 mm.
  • At 2.0% shrinkage, the part measures 99.74 mm.

That is a swing of about ±0.25 mm, more than the ±0.17 mm commercial tolerance for that dimension. That is why tight tolerances in PP require locking the resin grade, stabilizing the process and often adjusting the mold after the first trial.

What does ISO 20457 cover?

ISO 20457:2018 defines the manufacturing tolerances achievable on molded plastic parts and their acceptance conditions. It covers general tolerances on part features and surface profile tolerances within a datum system. It applies to injection, injection-compression, transfer, compression and rotational molding.

In Germany, its adoption as DIN ISO 20457 replaced the older DIN 16742. The standard does not override tolerances set in a product standard. It is a paid document and is under review, so if your drawing cites it, confirm the current edition with your molder.

What makes a tight tolerance expensive?

  • A more precise mold: more machining and fitting hours on critical dimensions.
  • Steel-safe adjustments: the mold is cut leaving extra steel on critical features, which is removed after measuring the first parts. Each adjustment is another trial round.
  • A narrower process window: less room on temperature, pressure and cooling, and sometimes a longer cycle.
  • More inspection and more scrap: more parts measured and more parts out of tolerance.
Common mistake

Putting ±0.05 mm (about ±0.002 in) as the general tolerance on the whole drawing. On a 100 mm PP part that is far below fine grade (±0.11 mm), raises the mold cost and makes the part fail a capability study, without making it work any better. Tighten only what matters.

Tolerances are decided together with the design. See the injection molding design rules and what a design for manufacturing review prevents. If your customer requires capability studies, the tolerance also decides whether the PPAP gets approved.
