Injection molding machine: parts, cycle and how to choose one

What an injection molding machine is, its main parts, how the molding cycle works and how to choose the right machine for your part.

7 min read · October 22, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

An injection molding machine melts plastic resin, injects it under pressure into a closed mold and holds it there until it cools and hardens. It has two main blocks: the injection unit, which melts and pushes the material, and the clamping unit, which holds the mold shut. To pick the right machine for a part you check four numbers: clamping force in tons, shot capacity, space between tie bars and mold height.

What is an injection molding machine?

It is the equipment that turns resin into finished parts. It takes plastic pellets, heats them until they flow, injects them into a mold cavity and lets them cool. Then it opens the mold, ejects the part and repeats. That is how everything from caps and pails to appliance parts gets made.

It is also called an injection press or injection molder. The machine does not define the shape of the part, the mold does. But the machine decides which parts you can run, how fast and how repeatably.

What are the parts of an injection molding machine?

They split into two units and a control system. The injection unit sits on one side, the clamping unit on the other, and the mold goes in between.

How does an injection molding machine work, step by step?

The cycle repeats every few seconds to a few minutes, depending on part size and wall thickness. These are the phases in order.

That is why cooling drives cycle time. A mold with good cooling runs faster, and that lowers the price per part.

Hydraulic, electric or hybrid?

The difference is how the screw, clamp and ejection move. None is best at everything. It depends on the part and the volume.

For general-purpose parts such as pails, containers or caps, a well-maintained hydraulic press is still a very solid choice. If the part needs tight tolerances and short cycles, an electric or hybrid machine usually performs better.

Which injection molding machine specs should you know?

A spec sheet has many numbers. These are the ones that decide whether a part fits and runs well.

Tonsclamping force: what the machine holds without the mold opening
Grams or ozshot capacity: the most material that fits in one shot
mm or intie-bar spacing: the maximum mold width and height
  • Clamping force: measured in tons. It depends on the part's projected area and the cavity pressure. It has its own guide: how to calculate injection molding clamping force, plus a clamp tonnage calculator to estimate it.
  • Shot capacity: the part weight plus the runner system should fit within a reasonable share of the machine's capacity. As an industry rule, the shot should sit in the middle range of the injection unit, not at either extreme.
  • Tie-bar spacing and platen size: the mold has to pass between the tie bars and sit securely on the platen.
  • Mold height and opening stroke: the mold must fall between the machine's minimum and maximum mold height, and the opening must let the part clear with room to spare.

How do you pick the right machine for a part?

Work in order. If a step does not fit, change the machine or rethink the mold before moving on.

Common mistake

Choosing a machine much bigger than needed "just in case." It costs more per hour, and with a small shot in a large screw the plastic sits hot too long and degrades. And a press that is barely enough in tonnage leaves the mold exposed to flash. Look for the middle point and confirm with a trial run. Machine upkeep matters too: see the key points in the Plastics Technology article in the sources.
