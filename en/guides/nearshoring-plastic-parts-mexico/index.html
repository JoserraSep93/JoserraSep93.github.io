Nearshoring plastic parts to Mexico: what a U.S. buyer gains

Lead time, inventory, tariffs and supply-chain risk: what changes when production moves from Asia to Mexico.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

The biggest gain for a U.S. buyer moving parts to Mexico is inventory and speed. In this guide's example, the supply chain drops from 9 weeks of inventory to just over 2, about 101,000 USD of working capital is freed, and an order change lands in 3 weeks instead of 9. The price and tariff comparison is covered in Mexico vs. China.

What is nearshoring and why are so many companies moving to Mexico?

Nearshoring means moving production to a country close to the market you sell in. If you sell in the United States, Mexico is the clearest case: a shared border, the same business hours and a trade agreement.

The numbers show it. According to the Congressional Research Service, Mexico was the top supplier of U.S. goods imports in 2025, at 534.8 billion USD, ahead of China and Canada.

This guide does not repeat the landed-cost comparison. It focuses on what rarely shows up in a quote: how much inventory you need, how much cash it ties up and how fast you can react when something changes.

How many weeks of inventory do you need with an Asian supplier vs. a Mexican one?

Supply chain inventory sits in three buckets, and all three grow with distance:

  • Pipeline inventory: parts you already paid for that are still moving. It equals usage times weeks in transit.
  • Cycle stock: the result of order size. If you order one container every 4 weeks, you hold about 2 weeks on average.
  • Safety stock: the buffer against swings in demand and delays. It grows with the square root of lead time and with lead-time variability.

Example assumptions (illustrative, not a quote):

  • Usage of 10,000 parts per week (520,000 a year), with weekly variation of 2,500 parts.
  • Asia: 4 weeks of production and 5 weeks door to door (Freightos puts ocean at 30 to 40 days). One 40,000-part container every 4 weeks. Lead-time variation: 1 week.
  • Mexico: 2 weeks of production and 1 week in transit, border crossing included. One 10,000-part truckload per week. Lead-time variation: 0.25 weeks.
  • 95% service level (z = 1.65) and the same 1.50 USD valuation per part in both cases, to isolate the inventory effect.

How much working capital do you free up by moving production to Mexico?

At the example valuation, the Asia supply chain ties up about 136,000 USD in parts. The Mexico one, about 34,900 USD. The difference, roughly 101,000 USD, is cash that comes back to the business once, when you resize the supply chain.

Then there is the cost of holding that inventory: capital, storage, insurance, handling and obsolescence. An APICS chapter gives 15% to 25% of inventory value per year as a rule of thumb. At 20%, the Asia supply chain costs about 27,200 USD a year in inventory and the Mexico one about 7,000 USD.

Why does safety stock grow so much with ocean freight?

Because the buffer covers more than demand. It also covers lead-time uncertainty. In the example, the 1-week variation in transit from Asia weighs more than all the demand variation combined.

56.4%of container vessels arrived on time in July 2026; late arrivals were delayed 6.06 days on averageSea-Intelligence

By truck from the Bajío, transit is measured in days and its variation in hours. That is why Mexico's buffer is less than half as large even with the same demand swings.

Common mistake

Moving production to Mexico and keeping the Asia buying policy: full-container orders, a reorder point built on 9 weeks and the same safety stock. You end up paying a nearby supplier while still carrying the inventory of a distant one. The benefit only shows up when you recalculate order size, frequency and buffer.

How fast can you react when demand changes?

A change to your order takes as long to reach your warehouse as the full lead time: production plus transit, plus waiting for the next scheduled shipment.

Example: demand jumps 25% without warning, to 12,500 parts per week. With Asia, the 20,600-part safety stock covers the gap for about 8 weeks and the adjusted order arrives in week 9: you are short about 1,900 parts or you pay for air freight. With Mexico, the 8,250-part buffer lasts about 3.3 weeks and the adjustment arrives in week 3. It is a simplified calculation, but it shows the order of magnitude.

When demand drops, the effect reverses: with Asia you are left holding 9 weeks of parts you already paid for.

What happens with a defective lot or an engineering change?

If you catch a defect at receiving, everything already on the way has the same problem. The same goes for a design change: the old inventory has to be used up or scrapped.

Proximity also shortens the fix: a plant visit to review the process or approve a sample takes days, in the same time zone. If the launch involves a new or transferred mold, see the injection molding project timeline.
