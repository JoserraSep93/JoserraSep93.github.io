How the price per part is calculated in injection molding

Resin, cycle time, machine rate, scrap, packaging and mold amortization: the full formula with an example.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Price per part = resin (including scrap) + press hourly rate divided by parts per hour + packaging + mold cost spread over volume + the molder's margin. On a mid-size PP part, press time and resin usually make up about two thirds of the price. That is why cycle time and part weight are the two strongest levers.

What is the formula for injection molding price per part?

DFMA, the firm behind one of the most widely used costing methods in engineering, breaks it into three terms: material cost, plus processing cost divided by parts per cycle, plus tooling cost divided by production volume. A contract manufacturer then adds packaging and its margin.

How much does an injection molding press cost per hour?

The rate rises with tonnage: a larger press costs more, uses more energy and takes more floor space. The rate covers operation, energy, maintenance and depreciation. These are the ranges DFMA publishes:

A part that could run on a 350-ton press but gets scheduled on a 650-ton press pays a higher rate for no benefit. Ask the molder which press it would run your part on and why. We cover how tonnage is sized in how many cavities your mold should have.

Worked example: a 125-gram PP part

Assumptions (illustrative example, not a quote):

  • Weight per part including its share of the runner: 125 g.
  • Colored PP: USD 1.75 per kg delivered to the plant. Resin prices move every month; confirm with your supplier.
  • Scrap: 3%.
  • 2-cavity mold, 36-second cycle, 85% plant efficiency.
  • 350-ton press at USD 55 per hour.
  • Packaging: USD 0.03 per part.
  • USD 40,000 mold amortized over 300,000 parts (100,000 a year for 3 years).
  • Molder margin: 15% over cost.

In DFMA's own example, an 18-gram ABS connector on a 4-cavity mold comes out at about USD 0.25 with a similar split: 37% material, 36% processing and 27% tooling. Parts like these tend to land in that order.

What moves the price per part the most?

Using the same assumptions, we changed one variable at a time:

Two takeaways. First, cycle time is driven mostly by cooling, and cooling is driven by wall thickness. A thinner, more uniform wall cuts cycle time and weight at once; Protolabs lists it among its first tips to reduce cost. We go deeper in injection molding design rules.

Second, the volume the mold is spread over moves the price more than any process tweak. If actual volume comes in at half, the price rises 18% in the example.

How does the price change with more cavities?

Using the same example with a USD 60,000 4-cavity mold, the part needs a larger press (USD 70 per hour) and the cycle rises to 38 seconds. Even so, output goes from 170 to 322 parts per hour and the price drops from USD 0.82 to 0.77. The mold costs USD 20,000 more, but 300,000 parts pay for it. The decision rule is in how many cavities your mold should have.

Why does the price change from one order to the next?

Mostly because of resin. Its price moves every month with oil, natural gas and producer supply. That is why many quotes include an adjustment clause: if resin moves up or down by more than an agreed percentage, the price per part is adjusted in proportion to resin's share of cost.

In the example, resin is 28% of the price. A 10% rise in resin moves the price per part by about 3%. Ask that the clause name the reference index used and how often it is reviewed.

Should you pay for the mold up front or build it into the part price?

Both are common. Paying for the mold separately drops the example price from USD 0.82 to 0.67 and the tool is paid off from day one. Amortizing it into the price avoids the up-front outlay, but the molder is financing the tool, so the contract should say what happens if volume falls short.

Either way, put mold ownership in writing. We explain how in how to protect your design when outsourcing. To understand what drives tooling cost, see how much an injection mold costs.

Common mistake

Comparing two quotes on the bottom-line price alone. One may amortize the mold over 100,000 parts and the other over 500,000; one may include packaging and the other may not. Ask each supplier for part weight, cycle time, cavities, press size and amortization volume. With those five numbers the quotes become comparable.

What does the price per part leave out?

  • Freight and duties: they depend on the Incoterm. See Incoterms for shipping parts from Mexico.
  • Special quality documentation: a full PPAP or capability studies are quoted separately.
  • Secondary operations: assembly, inserts or labels add their own labor and material.
  • Design changes: modifying the mold after approval is a separate cost.

How can I lower the price without changing suppliers?

  • Review wall thickness with the molder before the mold is built: it lowers cycle time and resin.
  • Give a realistic annual volume: amortization and cavity count are based on it.
  • Consolidate orders: fewer mold and color changes mean less startup scrap.
  • Consider recycled resin if the application allows it.
