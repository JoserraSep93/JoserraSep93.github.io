Injection molding defects: causes and how to fix them

Sink marks, flash, warpage, weld lines and short shots: how to recognize each defect, where it comes from and who fixes it (design, mold or process).

7 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Almost every defect in an injection molded part comes from one of three places: the part design, the mold or the process settings. Sink marks, flash, warpage, weld lines and short shots look different, but each has a dominant cause. Once you know which one it is, you know who fixes it and how long it will take. A process adjustment is made at the press. A mold change means pulling the mold and modifying it. A design change comes back to you.

How do you recognize each defect?

Name what you see before you look for causes. The same symptom under the wrong name sends everyone to fix the wrong thing.

  • Sink mark: a depression on the visible face, almost always over a thick area, a rib or a boss.
  • Flash: a thin film of plastic sticking out at the parting line, the ejector pins or the inserts.
  • Warpage: the part comes out twisted or curved and does not sit flat, even when each local dimension looks right.
  • Weld line: a visible line where two flow fronts met and did not bond well. It shows up behind holes and behind ribs that split the flow.
  • Short shot: the plastic did not fill a section, usually the one farthest from the gate or the thinnest one.

What gets fixed in the part design?

Sink marks and warpage are prevented mostly in design, before any steel is cut. Plastic cools from the outside in. If the outer skin has already solidified while the core of a thick area is still shrinking, the shrinkage pulls the surface inward and a sink mark forms.

Protolabs recommends that ribs and gussets be no more than 60% of the nominal thickness of the wall that supports them. It also notes that a section around 0.150 in. (3.8 mm) can already cool unevenly and sink on itself. Other guides, such as Xometry's, cite ranges of 50 to 70% depending on the material.

Other design measures that reduce defects: uniform walls, smooth transitions from thick to thin, radii on corners and coring out the back of a thick area when the sink mark shows on the visible face. The full set of rules is in the guide to injection molding design rules.

What gets fixed in the mold?

  • Gate location and size: they set the path of the plastic and where the fronts meet. Moving the gate can take a weld line out of a visible area.
  • Venting: if the air cannot escape, the part comes out short or with burn marks.
  • Cooling: poorly spread channels cool one side more than the other and cause warpage.
  • Parting line and fit: a worn mold lets plastic through. That is why preventive maintenance matters, as explained in the guide to mold life and maintenance.

A mold modification means taking it off the press and sending it to the tool room. It is slower and costlier than a process adjustment, so the first move is to try to solve the defect at the press whenever the cause is in the process.

What gets fixed in the process?

The settings that move most are packing pressure and time, injection speed, melt temperature, mold temperature and cooling time. With them you fix many sink marks, weld lines and short shots.

One cross effect is worth knowing. A short shot is often attacked with more pressure or more material, and that can create flash. Fixing one defect at the cost of another is a sign that the cause sits in the mold or the design, not at the press. With too little tonnage the mold also opens and flash appears; the calculation is in the guide to clamping force.

Common mistake

Accepting a sample with a minor defect because it will be fixed in production. A sink mark or weld line that is already in the first sample almost never disappears with process tweaks. Agree with your supplier on which cosmetic defects are acceptable, in which areas and against which limit sample, before approval. That agreement is documented with the first sample, as we cover in the guide to PPAP and first article inspection.

What should you ask a molder about defects?

  • How do you classify and record defects by cavity, machine and shift?
  • Is there an approved limit sample for cosmetic defects?
  • Who authorizes a parameter change and how is it documented?
  • How do you coordinate a mold modification when the cause is there?

These answers connect to the full quality system, which we describe in the guide to quality control for plastic parts.
