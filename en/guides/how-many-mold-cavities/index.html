How many cavities your mold should have

How volume, investment and cost per part intersect, with a step-by-step numeric example.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Use as many cavities as your volume pays for. As a starting point: under 50,000 parts a year, 1 or 2 cavities; 50,000 to 500,000, 4 to 8; above 500,000, 8 or more. The final number comes from comparing total cost per part and checking that the press has the tonnage for every cavity.

What is a cavity and why does it change cost per part?

A cavity is the part-shaped void in the mold. A 4-cavity mold makes 4 parts every cycle. If the cycle runs about as long, each press hour is spread over four times as many parts.

The tradeoff is a more expensive mold: more steel, more machining and a runner system that must fill every cavity evenly. Protolabs puts it this way: the mold costs more up front, and the per-part price drops thanks to higher output and shorter cycle time per part.

How many cavities for your annual volume?

1 to 2cavities for under 50,000 parts a year
4 to 8cavities for 50,000 to 500,000 parts a year
8 to 32cavities for over 500,000 parts a year

These are industry rules of thumb (RJC Mold). They start the conversation; they do not settle it. Part size changes everything: a large bucket rarely runs more than 1 or 2 cavities, while a small cap can run 16 or more.

How do you calculate the number of cavities?

Example: cost per part with 1, 2, 4 and 8 cavities

Take a PP part with 23 in² of projected area. At about 2.5 tons per in², each cavity needs roughly 60 tons of clamp force. As cavities increase, so do the press size and its hourly rate: 8 cavities need 480 tons, and with the runner and a 10 to 15% margin you are on a 650-ton press.

Example assumptions (illustrative, not a quote):

  • Resin: $0.10 per part, the same for every option.
  • Cycle: 30 s with 1 and 2 cavities, 31.5 s with 4 and 33 s with 8 (cooling more steel stretches the cycle slightly).
  • Plant efficiency: 85%.
  • Mold: $25,000 (1 cavity), $38,000 (2), $58,000 (4) and $90,000 (8).
  • Press rate: $35/h (125 t), $40/h (160 t), $50/h (320 t) and $70/h (650 t).

The reading is straightforward. At 100,000 parts, 1 and 2 cavities cost about the same and the pricier tools lose. At 500,000, the 4-cavity mold wins. At 2 million, the 8-cavity mold wins because its investment is spread over enough parts.

At what volume do more cavities pay off?

The break-even point is the volume at which savings per part cover the difference in mold price. The formula: difference in mold cost divided by savings per part (resin plus press time).

In the example, going from 1 to 2 cavities costs $13,000 more and saves $0.147 per part. $13,000 / $0.147 = about 88,000 parts.

4,902 hof press time for 500,000 parts with 1 cavity
674 hof press time for the same parts with 8 cavities
62,500mold cycles for 500,000 parts with 8 cavities (vs. 500,000 with 1)

Press time matters too: with 1 cavity, 500,000 parts tie up a press for more than half a year running three shifts. If demand is seasonal or the launch is urgent, more cavities give you room to react. The mold also runs fewer cycles for the same volume, so it can be a less expensive SPI class.

What limits the number of cavities?

  • Tonnage: all cavities together must fit within the press clamp force, with a 10 to 15% margin.
  • Shot size: the volume of all parts plus runner must fit in the barrel's shot capacity.
  • Physical size: the mold has to fit between the press tie bars.
  • Fill balance: every cavity must fill the same way. Poor balance gives parts with different dimensions depending on the cavity.
Common mistake

Ordering 8 cavities "to make it cheaper" without the volume to back it. In the example, at 100,000 parts the 8-cavity mold costs $1.09 per part, 58% more than the single-cavity tool. More cavities do not fix a bad design either: if the part has fill problems, you get them eight times over.

Multi-cavity or family mold?

A multi-cavity mold makes several copies of the same part. A family mold makes different parts of one product in a single tool, such as a lid and its base. A family mold saves investment when the parts share resin and color and are similar in size. If one part outsells the other, you end up with surplus of the slower one.

What data do you need to decide?

  • Annual volume and expected product life in years.
  • The part's 3D file, to measure projected area and volume.
  • Resin and color.
  • Demand peaks or launch dates.

With that, you can compare 2 or 3 cavity options. If something is missing, see what we need to quote.
