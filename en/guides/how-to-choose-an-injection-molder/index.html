How to choose an injection molder: 12 questions to ask

The questions that separate a reliable supplier from an expensive mistake, and the answer to expect for each one.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Choose the molder that can prove, with documents and a plant visit, that it has the right press for your part, a quality system you can verify, control over the mold and a price with clear assumptions. The 12 questions in this guide tell you which answer to expect and which answer should raise a red flag.

What should I check before asking a molder for a quote?

Three quick filters save weeks. First, its press range must cover your part size. Second, it must have real experience with your resin. Third, its certification must be current and verifiable. If any of them fails, there is no point in going further.

The first figure tells you something important: ISO 9001 is the baseline, not a differentiator. There are more than a million certificates in force. What sets a good supplier apart is how that system works on your part.

What are the 12 questions I should ask?

How do I know the molder has the right press?

The press is chosen by the clamping force the part needs, which depends on its projected area and the resin. Ask what tonnage they calculated and which press they would use. A part on an oversized press pays a higher rate for nothing; on an undersized one, it comes out with flash or short shots.

Also ask about a backup press. If your part fits only one press in the plant, a breakdown leaves you without product. We explain the tonnage calculation in how many cavities your mold should have.

How do I verify an ISO 9001 or IATF 16949 certification?

Ask for the certificate number and the name of the issuing body. Accredited ISO 9001, ISO 14001 and ISO 45001 certificates can be searched by company name or number on IAF CertSearch. IATF 16949, the automotive standard, is checked in the IATF database.

Check the scope: a certificate may cover only one plant or only certain processes. If your part is automotive, your customer may require IATF 16949; for medical devices, ISO 13485. For most other industries, ISO 9001 is the common reference.

Common mistake

Choosing the lowest price per part without seeing how it was built. A cheap quote may amortize the mold over a volume you are unlikely to order, or leave packaging out. Ask for the assumptions and compare them with the formula in how the price per part is calculated.

What should be clear about the mold?

Three things in writing: who builds it, who owns it and what happens to it if you change suppliers. The mold is often the most expensive asset in the project. A good molder tags it, logs its maintenance and does not put obstacles in the way of returning it. We cover this in how to protect your design when outsourcing and in injection mold life and maintenance.

What should I look for on a plant visit?

A half-day visit tells you more than ten slide decks. Bring your drawing and look for evidence, not promises:

  • Presses running: how many are running and how many are idle. Ask why the idle ones are down.
  • Resin storage: sealed bags, labeled by lot and off the floor. Drying of moisture-sensitive resins should be logged.
  • Mold area: molds tagged, protected from rust and with their maintenance history.
  • Inspection: the criteria sheet for a real part and the records from the last shift.
  • Traceability: ask them to trace a packed carton back to its resin lot, press and shift.
  • Housekeeping: a cluttered plant tends to have contamination and mixed-part problems.

Which supplier risks should I check beyond quality?

A good supplier can fail for reasons you will not see in the part. Ask what share of its sales depends on a single customer: if it is very high, losing that customer can disrupt its operation. Check how long it has been in business, whether it is backed by a larger group and how it finances molds.

Ask about continuity as well: what happens if the main press goes down, the power goes out or resin arrives late. The answer should include a second compatible press, safety stock or an alternate resin source.
