Injection molding design rules: wall thickness, draft, radii and ribs

The reference values an engineer applies before sending a part to tooling, with diagrams and examples.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Keep walls uniform and inside your resin's range. Use at least 0.5° of draft and 1° where the design allows, plus 1° for every 0.001 in (0.025 mm) of texture depth. Make ribs 40 to 60% of the wall and no taller than 3 times their base. Size boss outside diameters at 2 to 2.4 times the screw or insert. Make the inside radius at least half the wall and the outside radius equal to the inside radius plus the wall.

These are the values an engineer applies before sending a part to tooling. We took them from resin manufacturer and molder design guides and organized them by resin and by feature. To understand which defects they prevent and what late fixes cost, start with what a design for manufacturing review is.

What wall thickness and draft angle should you use for each resin?

Walls should be even: Fictiv recommends staying within ±10% of nominal thickness. Semi-rigid resins such as PP and HDPE tolerate less draft than rigid ones such as ABS and PC, because they flex slightly during ejection.

If you have not chosen a resin yet, start with how to choose a resin for an injection-molded part. If you plan to use glass fiber, read what changes with glass-filled plastics, since shrinkage and mold wear change.

How thick can a rib be before it shows sink?

Covestro publishes maximum rib thickness for each resin family at two levels: minimal sink and slight sink. Filled resins accept thicker ribs because they shrink less. On parts with walls of 1 mm (0.04 in) or less, the rib can equal the wall, because a thinner one will not fill.

How do you size a rib?

Example: an ABS housing with a 2.5 mm (0.10 in) wall. The rib base runs 1.0 to 1.5 mm; at 1.25 mm, the recommended height reaches about 3.75 mm. With 0.5° per side, the tip ends at 1.19 mm, still easy to fill. Parallel ribs sit at least 5 mm apart with a 0.6 to 1.25 mm base fillet. Other guides allow height up to 3 times the wall (7.5 mm); if you need that, check it in a fill simulation.

Common mistake

Drawing the rib as thick as the wall "so it holds more." The rib-to-wall junction becomes a thick spot that cools last and leaves a sink mark on the visible face. If the part needs more strength, add thinner ribs or change the resin before making anything thicker.

How much draft does a textured part need?

Covestro's rule is 1° as a base plus 1° for every 0.001 in (0.025 mm) of texture depth. Xcentric uses the same rule. Fictiv recommends 2° to 3° as a general draft where the design allows, and Covestro calls for 3° to 5° on resins such as TPU.

Draft shows up in the dimensions. On a 2 in (50 mm) deep wall, each degree moves the face almost 1 mm between base and tip. Draw the part with draft from the start so the drawing's dimensions already include it.

How do you design a boss for a screw or insert?

  • Outside diameter: 2.0 to 2.4 times the outside diameter of the screw or insert (Covestro).
  • Boss wall: the same ratio to nominal wall as ribs (Covestro); Fictiv gives 60% of the wall.
  • Base fillet: about 0.015 in (0.38 mm) balances strength and appearance (Covestro).
  • Core hole: extend it down to the base-wall level, even if the screw does not need the depth, to avoid a thick spot.
  • Height: bosses taller than 5 times their outside diameter cause fill problems at the top or a thick section at the base.
  • Position: keep bosses away from sidewalls and tie them in with ribs instead of merging them into the wall.

What radius should corners have?

The inside radius should be at least half the wall, and the outside radius should equal the inside radius plus one wall thickness. That keeps the wall even around the bend. With a 2 mm wall: 1 mm inside radius and 3 mm outside.

Covestro warns that sharp inside corners greatly reduce impact and fatigue strength, and gives a radius-to-thickness ratio of about 0.15 as a reasonable floor for light to moderate impact loads. It also recommends calling out the radius on the drawing as a range, not a maximum: a maximum lets the shop leave the corner sharp, at under 0.005 in (0.13 mm). On non-critical outside corners, avoid blanket radius callouts, which add mold cost without improving the part.

What else should you check before releasing the design?

  • Thickness transitions: rounded or tapered; avoid square steps.
  • Undercuts: external ones are handled with side actions and are the most economical; internal ones need more expensive inserts or mechanisms (Protolabs). The price effect is covered in how much an injection mold costs.
  • Tolerances: tighten only what matters; typical values by resin are in injection molding tolerances.
