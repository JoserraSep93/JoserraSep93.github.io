Forecast, safety stock and blanket orders with your contract molder

How to share your forecast, how much safety stock makes sense and how a blanket order lowers price per part and stockout risk.

6 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Share a weekly or monthly forecast with your molder, with a horizon of several months, and update it on a regular schedule. Safety stock comes from a standard formula: Z × standard deviation of demand × square root of the replenishment lead time. A blanket order sets a volume for a period and releases it in partial deliveries, which gives the supplier visibility to plan press time and resin, and gives you availability without paying for a huge warehouse.

Why does a forecast matter if I already send purchase orders?

A purchase order tells your molder what you want on one date. A forecast tells them what you will need over the coming months. With it they plan press time, buy resin and schedule the mold. Without one, every order competes for the same press with other customers' jobs, and resin gets ordered when it is already urgent.

A forecast is not a purchase commitment. It is an intention with a margin of error, and it works when both sides treat it that way. To see which quantities make sense per run, read the guide on minimum volume in injection molding.

How do I share a forecast the molder can use?

What is safety stock and how is it calculated?

It is the cushion of finished parts (or resin) that absorbs variation in demand and replenishment time. It is not for normal demand, only for what deviates from it. The standard formula, when demand varies and lead time is stable, is:

Safety stock = Z × σd × √L

  • Z: the service level factor. It is the probability of not running out of parts during a replenishment cycle.
  • σd: standard deviation of demand per period (per week, for example), taken from your history.
  • L: replenishment lead time, in the same periods as σd.

If lead time also varies, the combined version is Z × √(L × σd² + d² × σL²), where d is average demand per period and σL is the standard deviation of lead time. IBM documents the variants of the formula depending on which variable moves.

Worked example, step by step

This is an illustrative example with made-up figures to show the method, not data from any customer. One part with average demand of 2,000 pieces per week, a standard deviation of 400 pieces per week and a 3-week replenishment lead time. You want a 95% service level.

Now assume lead time also varies, with a standard deviation of half a week. The combined formula gives √(3 × 400² + 2,000² × 0.5²) = √1,480,000 = 1,217, and 1.65 × 1,217 = 2,007 pieces. Moderate uncertainty in lead time nearly doubles the cushion, which is why it pays to agree on stable lead times before asking for more stock.

What is a blanket order and how does it work?

A blanket order is a purchase order for a total volume over a period, with several partial deliveries that you release as you need them. The reference source describes it as an order for multiple deliveries over time, usually at prices agreed in advance, filled until the period ends or the volume runs out.

How does it lower price and stockout risk?

  • Price per part: with annual volume in sight, the molder can spread setups, mold changes and resin purchases over more parts. How that price is built is in the guide on price per part in injection molding.
  • Stockouts: the plant reserves press time and buys resin ahead of time instead of reacting to urgent orders.
  • Cash: you receive in tranches and do not carry the whole volume in your warehouse from day one.
What to watch in a blanket order

Put in writing what happens if your consumption falls below the agreed volume, how many weeks ahead are firm and which are forecast only, and who absorbs resin already bought if you cancel. A blanket order without those rules breeds disputes. Releases inside the firm horizon are treated as orders and later ones as forecasts that can still move; that is how supplier scheduling systems describe it.

How do I combine forecast, safety stock and a blanket order?

They work as a set. The forecast provides visibility, the blanket order provides the commercial frame and safety stock covers what the forecast does not anticipate. A sensible sequence is to start by sharing the forecast, calculate safety stock from your history and then formalize the blanket order.
