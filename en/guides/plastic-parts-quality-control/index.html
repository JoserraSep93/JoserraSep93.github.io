Quality control for plastic parts: inspection, sampling and reports

First-piece, in-process and final inspection, AQL sampling with an example and which report to ask for.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Quality control for a molded part happens at three points: first-piece inspection before production is released, in-process checks at the frequency set by the control plan, and final lot inspection with AQL sampling. An ISO 2859-1 example: from a lot of 5,000 parts you inspect 200; at AQL 2.5 for major defects, the lot is accepted with 10 or fewer defective parts and rejected at 11.

What defects do you look for in an injection-molded part?

Most molding rejects come down to four defects. Knowing them helps you write clear acceptance criteria and read an inspection report.

Inspectors also check color against the approved sample, part weight and functional dimensions. Weight is a cheap and useful check: if it drops, it flags incomplete fill before it is visible.

What are first-piece, in-process and final inspection?

The in-process frequency is set by each part's control plan. As an illustrative example, a part with one critical dimension might be checked with 5 parts per cavity every 2 hours, while a purely cosmetic part might get one visual check per shift. The criteria are agreed with the customer based on risk.

How does ISO 2859-1 AQL sampling work?

Inspecting 100% of a large lot is expensive, and with repetitive visual checks it is not flawless either. ISO 2859-1 defines how many parts to inspect and how many defects to tolerate, based on three inputs:

  • Lot size: parts made under the same conditions.
  • Inspection level: general levels I, II and III, plus special levels S-1 to S-4 for destructive or slow tests. Level II is the most common.
  • AQL: the acceptance quality limit for each defect class: critical, major and minor.

Lot size and level give you the code letter in the first table. The code letter and AQL give you the sample size and the accept and reject numbers in the second table.

Example: accepting or rejecting a lot of 5,000 caps

Assumptions: a lot of 5,000 molded caps, normal inspection, general Level II. For a lot of 3,201 to 10,000 parts the code letter is L, which means a 200-part sample. Customer and supplier agreed on these AQLs:

200parts inspected out of 5,000 (4% of the lot)
7major defects found: accepted, the limit is 10
12minor defects found: accepted, the limit is 14

The lot passes. If the same sample had turned up 11 major defects, the whole lot would be rejected even with minor defects within limits. What happens next (100% sorting, rework or replacement) should be set in the quality agreement ahead of time, not at the moment of rejection.

The standard also includes switching rules: tightened inspection when several lots in a row fail, and reduced inspection when the supplier has a good track record. The third edition, ISO 2859-1:2026, replaced the 1999 edition in January 2026; the numbers in this example come from the tables the industry uses, so confirm them against your current copy. In the United States, ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 is also widely used, with equivalent tables.

Common mistake

Assuming AQL 2.5 means the lot contains at most 2.5% bad parts. The AQL is the worst process average tolerated over time; a lot with somewhat more than 2.5% defects can still be accepted. If a defect is unacceptable to you, classify it as critical and agree on zero accepted or 100% inspection.

Which quality report should you ask your supplier for?

  • With every shipment: a certificate of conformance with lot number, sample size, AQL applied and defects found by class.
  • For material: the resin manufacturer's lot certificate.
  • Dimensional: results on the control plan's critical dimensions, by cavity.
  • Traceability: date, press and shift for each lot, so any complaint is narrowed to what is actually affected.

Set these criteria before production, together with first article or PPAP approval and the drawing tolerances. If you are evaluating suppliers, ask how they control each of the three points; it belongs in the 12 questions for choosing a molder.
