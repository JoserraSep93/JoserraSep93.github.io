Injection mold: what it is, its parts and types

What an injection mold is, its parts (cavity, core, runners, gates, cooling, ejectors), the mold types, steel classes, who owns it and what to ask before ordering one.

8 min read · October 22, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

An injection mold is a steel (or aluminum) tool with a cavity shaped like your part. The press injects molten plastic, the mold cools it and ejects it solid. Its key parts are the cavity and core, the filling system (sprue, runners and gates), cooling and the ejectors. Mold types differ in how they fill (cold or hot runner), how many parts they make per cycle and how long they last. Before ordering one, settle who owns it, which steel it uses and how many cycles it must survive.

What is an injection mold and what is it for?

It is the tool that gives the plastic its shape. It mounts in an injection press, which closes the two mold halves, injects molten resin under pressure, waits for it to cool and opens to release the part. That cycle repeats thousands or millions of times with the same shape and the same dimensions.

That is why the mold is the biggest investment in an injection project: once it is paid for, each additional part is cheap. What it costs and what drives that price is covered in our injection mold cost guide.

What are the mold types by how they fill?

The filling system is the most visible difference between molds. There are three common setups:

The two-plate mold is the most common and the easiest to maintain. The three-plate is used when the gate must sit at the center of the part without paying for a hot system. The hot runner removes waste and shortens the cycle, but costs more and needs more maintenance. To choose between cold and hot, read the hot runner vs cold runner guide.

What other mold types are there?

  • Multi-cavity mold: makes several copies of the same part per cycle. It lowers cost per part when volume pays for it. See how much it makes sense in the guide on how many cavities your mold needs.
  • Family mold: makes different parts of one product in a single mold. It saves investment, but all parts must use the same resin and color and have similar cycles.
  • Stack mold: has two or more levels of cavities one above the other. It doubles output per cycle without raising tonnage proportionally. It suits very high volumes and flat parts such as lids and thin-wall containers.
  • Mold with inserts or overmolding: a metal or plastic piece is placed in the mold and plastic is injected over it, or a second material is injected over the first. It is used for screw joints, soft-grip handles or seals. We have a guide on overmolding and inserts.
  • Prototype or production mold: the prototype mold (usually aluminum) validates the design and makes a few parts. The production mold, in steel, is built to run hundreds of thousands of cycles. The aluminum vs steel molds guide explains when each one fits.

What do the steel classes mean, in plain terms?

The mold material decides how many cycles it lasts before wearing out. In practice you will see these options:

  • Aluminum: machines quickly and costs less. It lasts fewer cycles. The typical choice for prototypes and short runs.
  • Pre-hardened steel (for example P20): arrives with medium hardness. The middle ground between cost and life. Good for medium volumes.
  • Hardened steel (for example H13 or S7): heat treated to resist wear. For high-volume production and abrasive resins, such as glass-filled grades.
  • Stainless steel (for example 420): resists corrosion. Chosen for resins that release corrosive gases, such as PVC, or for humid environments.

The industry classifies molds by expected life with SPI classes 101 to 105. Class 101 is for continuous production of millions of cycles and 105 for prototypes and very short runs. How many cycles to expect and how to care for them is in the guide on mold life and maintenance.

Common mistake

Buying an aluminum or soft steel mold for a part that will run for years. The upfront savings disappear when the mold wears out and has to be repaired or rebuilt halfway through the program. Choose the steel based on the cycles you will really run, not only on the entry price.

Who owns the mold and why does it matter?

It is one of the questions most often left unwritten. The mold can be paid for by the customer, the manufacturer or both, and that decides who can take it away, who can use it for another customer and who is responsible for its maintenance.

The healthy practice is that the mold you paid for is yours, is tagged with your name and can be removed whenever you decide. Ask for it in writing in the quote or the contract. If you ever change suppliers, that clarity saves months of dispute. If you already have a mold in another country, read the guide to transferring a mold to Mexico.

Who can help you with the mold?

If you are not a tooling specialist, you do not need to be one. What matters is that whoever makes your parts can review the mold, mount it and run it, and tell you with data if something needs correcting before production.
