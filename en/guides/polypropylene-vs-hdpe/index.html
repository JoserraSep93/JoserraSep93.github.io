Polypropylene vs. HDPE: when to use each one

Properties side by side, typical applications and a quick guide to choosing the right resin.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Use polypropylene (PP) when the part needs stiffness, heat resistance or a molded-in living hinge. Use high-density polyethylene (HDPE) when it will see cold temperatures, impacts or long-term chemical contact. If the part needs both, a PP impact copolymer covers most cases.

PP and HDPE are the two most common resins for general-purpose injection molded parts. They run on the same presses and look almost identical on the shelf. That is why many buyers pick one out of habit and only find the problem when a part cracks in the field or warps in a dishwasher.

This guide compares the two using real datasheet numbers for injection-grade resins, so you can make the call on data instead of habit.

What is the main difference between PP and HDPE?

Both are semi-crystalline polyolefins: chains of carbon and hydrogen that form ordered regions (crystals) as they cool. The difference is a methyl group that PP carries on its chain and HDPE does not. That small detail makes PP stiffer and more heat resistant, and makes HDPE tougher in the cold and slightly more stable against chemicals.

The table uses three LyondellBasell datasheets, one of the largest polyolefin producers. These are typical properties for a specific grade, not specifications. Another grade in the same family can differ by 20 or 30% in stiffness or impact, so use them to compare and confirm against the datasheet of the exact grade you will buy.

Which one is stiffer?

PP homopolymer. At 1,450 MPa (210,000 psi) flexural modulus, a PP wall flexes less than the same wall in HDPE. That matters for lids that must close without bowing, trays that carry weight and housings that take screws.

Note the trade-off: the impact copolymer gains a lot of toughness but loses about 30% of the homopolymer's stiffness. Choosing PP is only half the decision; you also have to choose the type of PP.

Which one handles more heat?

PP. It melts around 160 °C (320 °F) versus 131 °C (268 °F) for HDPE, and its deflection temperature under load is about 20 °C (36 °F) higher in the datasheets compared. In practice, PP is the choice for parts that go through dishwashers, steam sterilization or hot water.

Which one holds up better in the cold and under impact?

HDPE. The pail grade in the table is not brittle even at -76 °C (-105 °F), and its room-temperature notched Izod is twice that of PP homopolymer (64 vs. 32 J/m). PP homopolymer turns brittle below freezing: a part that gets knocked around in a cold room or a northern winter can shatter.

If you need the stiffness and heat resistance of PP but the part works in the cold, the answer is a PP impact copolymer, which stays tough down to -40 °F at the cost of some stiffness.

Common mistake

Switching from HDPE to PP homopolymer to save resin on a part used outdoors in cold climates or in refrigeration. The part passes plant testing at 73 °F and fails in January. Test impact at the real service temperature.

Which one has better chemical resistance?

Both resist non-oxidizing acids and bases, most solvents at room temperature, greases and detergents. That is why both are used for chemical containers, paint pails and cleaning-product packaging.

The difference is one of degree: PP is somewhat less resistant than PE to strong oxidizers because of its chain structure. HDPE resists swelling from the aromatic components in fuels, which is why it is used for jerrycans and fuel tanks. If the part will contact a specific chemical, ask the resin producer for its compatibility chart and run an immersion test.

Which one is lighter, and how much do you save?

PP is one of the lightest commodity resins: 0.90 g/cm³ versus 0.953 for the HDPE in the table (HDPE ranges from 0.93 to 0.97 depending on grade). For the same part volume, PP weighs about 6% less.

Example: a 500 cm³ (30.5 in³) part weighs 450 g (0.99 lb) in PP and 477 g (1.05 lb) in HDPE. Over 100,000 parts that is 2.7 metric tons (about 5,950 lb) of resin. At similar prices per pound, that 6% goes straight to your part cost; when prices differ, run the math with the current month's quote, because the two resins move independently.

Which one shrinks more in the mold?

HDPE. Both resins shrink much more than amorphous materials such as ABS or PC, because the crystals pack tighter as they cool. HDPE shrinks 1.5 to 3.0% and PP 1.0 to 2.5%. More shrinkage means more risk of warpage and sink marks on thick walls.

This has a practical consequence: a mold is sized for one resin. A mold cut for PP can produce out-of-tolerance parts if you shoot HDPE, and vice versa. Changing resin in an existing mold calls for a dimensional study before you release production. We cover this in more depth in the design for manufacturing review guide.
