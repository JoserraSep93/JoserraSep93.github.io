Hot runner vs. cold runner: cost, benefits and when each makes sense

Investment, cycle time, runner scrap and part quality: how to choose the mold's feed system.

6 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A hot runner makes the mold more expensive but eliminates the runner you throw away or regrind, and it usually shortens the cycle. It pays off at high volume, on small parts with heavy runners and with expensive resins or resins that cannot take regrind. A cold runner fits low volume, frequent color changes and tight tooling budgets. In this guide's example, at a million parts a year a hot runner pays back in about 10 months; at 100,000 parts it does not pay back.

What is a cold runner and what is a hot runner?

The runner is the path molten plastic travels from the press nozzle to each cavity. In a cold runner mold that path cools along with the part and comes out as a plastic "tree" that must be separated, scrapped or reground.

In a hot runner mold, a heated manifold keeps the plastic molten up to each cavity gate. Only the part comes out. Husky puts it this way: the part cools and is ready to eject, while a cold runner has to cool before the mold can open.

What are the pros and cons of each?

How much material does a cold runner waste?

It depends on runner weight relative to the part. With small parts and several cavities, the runner can weigh as much as the parts or more. Some of it can be reground and reused, within limits: UL Prospector reports that molders target 20 to 25% regrind in the blend, or less.

about 80%of the cycle is cooling (MoldMaking Technology)
100% virginis required in medical, so the whole runner is scrap (Mold-Masters)

Every remelt adds heat history and can cost properties. That is why engineering resins tolerate less regrind. For more, see recycled plastic in injection-molded parts.

Example: what a hot runner saves in scrap and cycle time

Assumptions (illustrative, not a quote):

  • 25 g PP part, 4-cavity mold, 50 g cold runner per shot.
  • Volume: 1,000,000 parts a year, or 250,000 shots.
  • Resin: $1.40 per kg. Press: $45 per hour at 85% efficiency.
  • Cycle: 30 s with a cold runner and 26 s with a hot runner.
  • Hot runner: $16,000 more on the mold and $1,500 more per year in maintenance.

If the part cannot take regrind (medical contact, critical color, sensitive resin), material savings rise to $17,500 and payback drops to about 6 months. At low volume the math changes completely.

When does a hot runner make sense?

  • High annual volume and a product with several years of life.
  • Small parts in high-cavitation molds, where the runner is heavy compared with the part.
  • Expensive resins or resins that cannot take regrind.
  • Parts where the gate mark matters: valve gates leave a minimal vestige, per Husky.
  • Cycles limited by cooling a thick runner.

A hot runner also changes the math on how many cavities your mold should have, because it cuts material per shot and the runner's share of projected area.

Is there a middle option?

Yes. For volumes that do not justify a full system, MoldMaking Technology suggests a hot-to-cold combination or at least a heated sprue bushing. The sprue is the thickest part of the runner; heating it cuts cycle, resin and scrap for a smaller investment. Another option is a hot manifold feeding short cold sub-runners, with fewer nozzles.

Common mistake

Specifying a hot runner "because it is better" without running the numbers. At 100,000 parts a year, the example takes about 24 years to pay back, and the mold adds heaters and thermocouples to maintain. The flip side: skipping it on a multi-million-part program to save on the mold, then paying for runner scrap every year.

What should the mold quote state?

  • Runner type: cold, hot or hybrid, and number of drops.
  • Gate type: thermal or valve.
  • Hot runner brand and spare parts availability.
  • Estimated runner weight per shot, if cold.
  • Estimated cycle time for each option.

Everything else that moves the price is in how much an injection mold costs, and system upkeep in mold life and maintenance.
