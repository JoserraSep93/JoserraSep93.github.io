Extrusion blow molding for containers: how it works and when to choose it

From parison to bottle: materials, wall thickness, sizes and what to ask when quoting a blow-molded container.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

In extrusion blow molding, a hot plastic tube is extruded, the mold pinches it shut and air inflates it against the cavity walls. It fits hollow containers from about 250 mL (8.5 oz) up to large tanks, with handles or offset necks, in HDPE or PP. The mold is usually aluminum and costs less than an injection blow mold. The things to watch are blow-up ratio, corners and the pinch-off area.

Chemical jugs, oil containers, cleaning-product bottles and agricultural containers are almost all made this way. This guide covers how the process works, which design rules prevent weak containers and what data to send when you request a quote.

How does extrusion blow molding work?

Extrusion, injection or stretch blow molding?

They are three different processes. Injection blow molding first injects a preform and then blows it; it suits small bottles with a very precise neck. Stretch blow molding stretches the preform with a rod before blowing and is the standard process for PET beverage bottles. Extrusion blow molding is the most common and the most flexible in shape.

Which resin works best for a blow-molded container?

High-density polyethylene (HDPE) is the most used resin in blow molding: Belcher put it at more than 57% of the market. It is stiff, resists many household and agricultural chemicals and regrinds well. PP offers a somewhat higher heat deflection temperature. LDPE makes flexible, squeezable containers.

If you are deciding between HDPE and PP, the full comparison is in polypropylene vs. HDPE. If the container will carry recycled content, read how much recycled plastic to use; the same property and color considerations apply.

What design rules apply to a blow-molded container?

  • Blow-up ratio: mold diameter divided by parison diameter. Belcher treats 4:1 as the maximum, for the whole container and for each section. A handle deeper than it is wide comes out thin and weak.
  • Depth versus width: Custom-Pak recommends that a feature formed between the two mold halves be no deeper than it is wide.
  • Rounded shapes: flat faces and sharp corners end up with uneven walls. Corners thin out, while flat panels thicken and distort.
  • Accent lines: use a radius of 1.5 mm (0.06 in) or more. Sharper lines keep the parison from reaching the bottom and leave trapped-air marks.
  • Ribs: in blow molding they do not always add stiffness. They can add surface area, thin the wall and create a bellows effect.
  • Pinch-off: the pinch mark can be a failure point. Keep loads and bearing surfaces away from it.
  • Texture: typical mold etch depth is 0.008 to 0.012 in (0.2 to 0.3 mm), per Custom-Pak.
Common mistake

Asking for "a 1-liter container" without saying whether that is nominal or brimful capacity, or how much it should weigh. Weight changes volume: Belcher reports that a 4 L bottle with 5 g less plastic gains about 12 mL of capacity, 5 from the missing plastic and 7 because the wall bulges. Define capacity, target weight and how long after molding it gets measured.

Why do the volume and dimensions of a blow-molded container change?

Because HDPE keeps shrinking after it leaves the mold, and 80 to 90% of that shrinkage happens in the first 24 hours. Storage temperature matters too: after 10 days, bottles stored at 60 °C (140 °F) change more than those stored at 20 °C (68 °F). Shorter cycles, lower blow pressure and lower melt and mold temperatures reduce container volume.

Wall thickness plays a role as well. Custom-Pak reports shrinkage of about 1.65% for a 0.060 in wall and 1.85% for a 0.125 in wall in PP and PE. That is why capacity and dimensions are best measured at 24 hours, against an agreed criterion.

What is a blow mold made of, and why does it cost less?

Blow molding runs at far lower pressures than injection molding, so the mold does not need hardened steel throughout. Most are machined from 7075 or 6061 aluminum; large industrial molds are cast in A356 aluminum. Harder beryllium-copper inserts go into the pinch-off area so the edge holds up.

Which tests does a blow-molded container go through?

The Society of the Plastics Industry published 21 standard practices for bottles. Belcher highlights five: vertical compression (top load), drop impact, product compatibility and permeability, closure torque and stress-crack resistance under load. Agree with your supplier on which ones apply to your container and how parts pass; the sampling logic is in quality control for plastic parts.
