USMCA rules of origin for plastic parts made in Mexico

When a part qualifies as originating, which document is needed and how much duty is saved.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A plastic part molded in Mexico (headings 39.16 to 39.26) qualifies as USMCA originating if its non-originating materials come from a different heading. Resin pellets are in a different heading, so the part qualifies even if the resin comes from Asia. With a certification of origin carrying the 9 minimum data elements, it enters the U.S. duty-free. Current as of October 2026; confirm with your customs broker.

What does it mean for a part to be USMCA originating?

Originating means the part meets the agreement's rules for preferential treatment: zero duty when it enters the United States or Canada. A "Made in Mexico" label is not enough. Article 4.2 of the USMCA sets out four ways to qualify.

What is the rule of origin for Chapter 39 plastic parts?

For headings 39.16 to 39.26, Annex 4-B of the USMCA reads: "A change to heading 39.16 through 39.26 from any other heading, including another heading within that group."

In practice: if your non-originating materials are classified in a different heading than the finished part, the part qualifies. Virgin resin in primary form sits in headings 39.01 to 39.15. Molding it in Mexico turns it into a part in a different heading, and that shift is what the rule asks for.

If a non-originating component does not make the heading shift, the part can still qualify when that component is worth 10% or less. In the fourth example, 0.08 divided by 1.20 is 6.7%.

Chapter 4 gives two more breaks: packing materials and containers for shipment (carton, pallet, stretch wrap) are disregarded for origin (Article 4.16), and retail packaging classified with the part is disregarded for the tariff shift (Article 4.15).

What document do you need to import duty-free?

A certification of origin. The USMCA dropped NAFTA's official form: it is self-certification, nothing is filed with an authority, and it can go on the invoice or any other document, provided it carries the 9 minimum data elements of Annex 5-A.

It may be written in English, Spanish or French. Whoever certifies must be able to prove origin with records: bill of materials, purchase invoices and the classification of every input.

How much duty does a Mexican plastic part save?

Most plastic articles (subheading 3926.90.99) and caps and closures (3923.50) carry a 5.3% general rate in the U.S. On top of that, since July 24, 2026, goods from Mexico that do not qualify under USMCA pay an additional 10% under Section 301. Qualifying parts are exempt from both.

To compare against a part made in China, where Section 301 adds 7.5% to 25% depending on the list plus 12.5% for forced labor, see manufacturing in Mexico or China.

The agreement remains in force, now with annual reviews, and expires on July 1, 2036 unless all three countries extend it first. Section 301 tariffs have changed several times this year. What are the most common certification mistakes?

  • Assuming "Made in Mexico" is enough. Country of origin for marking and USMCA origin are decided by different rules.
  • Misclassifying the part. The rule depends on the subheading; if the part classifies as a machine or vehicle part, a different rule may apply.
  • Certifying without support. If CBP verifies and there is no bill of materials showing the origin of each input, preference can be denied and duties collected for the period.
  • Letting the blanket period lapse. An expired 12-month certification leaves the next shipments uncovered.
