Mold finishes and textures: SPI, VDI and texturing

What SPI and VDI grades mean, how finish affects mold cost and part release, and how to specify it on your drawing.

7 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

The surface finish of an injection molded part is set by the mold; no later process changes it. The SPI scale (A to D) describes how the surface was worked: diamond for gloss, paper and stone for semi-gloss and matte, blasting for texture. The VDI 3400 scale describes EDM textures, from 12 (fine) to 45 (coarse). The glossier the finish or the deeper the texture, the more work for the toolmaker and, for texture, the more draft you need. Specify it by zone of the part, with a grade and a reference number, not with the word "nice."

What does a mold finish mean?

It is the surface texture of the cavity. The plastic copies that surface on every cycle, so the part finish is the mold finish. Protolabs states it plainly: polish and texture are applied directly to the mold and transferred to the part, so there is no secondary finishing step to fix it afterward.

That is why the finish should be decided before the mold is built. For where the cavity, core and ejectors sit, see the guide on mold parts and types.

How do you read the SPI scale?

The SPI scale (now the Plastics Industry Association) has 12 grades, from A-1 to D-3. The letter tells you the method and the number tells you how fine the result is within that family.

As a reference, Protolabs offers SPI-A2 (grade 2 diamond, high gloss), SPI-B1 (600-grit paper) and SPI-C1 (600-grit stone, a matte finish that only removes machining marks) as standard options. A C-1 comes out clean and without gloss; it does not reach a fine polish.

What is the VDI 3400 scale?

It is the German scale for textures made by electrical discharge machining (EDM). It runs from 12 to 45 and each step increases roughness. The reference relationship is Ra (µm) = 0.1 × 10^(VDI/20), which gives these approximate values:

Beyond SPI and VDI there are etched texture catalogs such as Mold-Tech, with hundreds of codes (sand, leather, wood, geometric patterns). They are applied by chemical etching or laser after the cavity is machined. In Protolabs examples, MT-11010 looks like sand and MT-11555 looks like wood. A drawing that says "MT-11010" is unambiguous; one that says "leather-like texture" is not.

How does the finish change mold cost?

The finish is mostly a one-time cost in the tool and usually does not move the price per part. What raises the bill is manual labor and risk:

  • High gloss (A grades): needs diamond polishing, a lot of skilled hours and good steel. Any scratch or inclusion shows.
  • Texture: is added after machining and needs a texturing vendor. If the texture is very light, the surface must first be brought to C-1 or even A-2 before it is etched.
  • Changing your mind later: as a rule of thumb, removing steel is easy and adding it is expensive. Going from glossy to matte is simple; going from texture back to gloss means reworking the cavity.

To see how much this weighs in the total, read how much an injection mold costs.

Why does texture need more draft?

Texture creates tiny vertical walls that grip the part when the mold opens. Without draft, the part scratches or drags. The Protolabs rule is one degree of draft for every 0.0005 to 0.0006 in (0.013 to 0.015 mm) of texture depth. A wood-grain or leather texture reaches about 0.003 in, which calls for 6 to 7 degrees.

A part with tall vertical walls and heavy texture needs design changes, not only a different finish. The rest of the geometry rules are in the injection molding design rules.

What else affects how the finish looks?

  • Material: hard plastics such as polycarbonate pick up texture better than soft ones. Unfilled nylon shows it more than nylon with 30% glass fiber.
  • Color: dark colors show texture more than light ones. See the guide on color matching and masterbatch.
  • Defects: high gloss shows weld lines, sink marks and flow marks that a texture hides. They are described in the guide on injection molding defects.
  • Deep ribs: sometimes the texturing does not reach the bottom of a narrow rib.

How do you specify it on your drawing?

Common mistake

Asking for mirror gloss on the whole part "just in case." You pay for polish on areas no one sees and make flow marks visible. Reserve the high grade for the visible face and leave C-1 or texture elsewhere.
