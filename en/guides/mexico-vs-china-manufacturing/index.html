Manufacturing in Mexico or China to sell in the United States

Total landed cost, transit times, tariffs and risk: the full comparison.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

As of October 2026, a plastic part molded in Mexico that qualifies as USMCA-originating enters the U.S. duty-free and arrives in days. The same part made in China can carry up to 42.8% in duties and takes 30 to 40 days door to door. In the example below, a Chinese supplier would need to cut its factory price by about 40% just to break even.

Why is the factory price misleading?

A supplier's quote tells you what the part costs on their dock. What you actually pay is the landed cost: factory price plus freight, duties, customs fees, insurance, brokers and the cash tied up in inventory while goods are on the water.

To compare fairly, build the full number with the same volume and the same destination. Here is an example with every assumption stated.

Assumptions: a technical polypropylene part, 20,000 parts per shipment, delivered to a warehouse in Dallas, Texas. Assumed factory price of $1.00 in China and $1.15 in Mexico (15% higher). Ocean freight of $8,100 per 40 ft container (the FBX01 index level in October 2026) plus $2,900 in port charges and drayage to Dallas. A truckload from Mexico to Dallas at $5,000 door to door. Cost of capital of 10% per year.

The useful question runs the other way: at what factory price does China break even? With these assumptions, the Chinese supplier would need to quote $0.59 instead of $1.00. That is 41% below the example price and roughly half the Mexican price. With $3,000 ocean freight, break-even is $0.77, or 23% lower.

What duties does a plastic part pay in October 2026?

The picture changed a lot this year. On February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the tariffs imposed under IEEPA (the so-called reciprocal and fentanyl tariffs). The temporary Section 122 global tariff that replaced them expired on July 24. That same day, a new Section 301 forced-labor tariff on 60 economies took effect.

0%Mexican part that qualifies as USMCA-originating
10%Added to Mexican parts that do not qualify (on top of the general rate)
12.5%Added to China under the Section 301 forced-labor action
7.5 to 25%Original Section 301 on China, depending on your HTS list

For most plastic articles (HTS heading 3926), the general duty rate is 5.3%. If your ten-digit HTS line is on Section 301 List 3, the total from China reaches 42.8%; on List 4A, 25.3%. The 178 remaining Section 301 exclusions expire in November 2026 unless extended.

Rates as of October 2026

Tariffs move often, and a separate Section 301 investigation on excess capacity is open that covers both China and Mexico. Confirm the rate for your exact HTS line with your customs broker before you decide. To enter duty-free, a Mexican part must meet the USMCA rule of origin. A part molded in Mexico from resin pellets usually meets the tariff-shift rule, but it is checked line by line.

How long does it take to get the goods?

From China, ocean freight takes 30 to 40 days door to door in normal conditions, and standard air takes 8 to 10 days at a much higher cost. From Mexico's Bajío region, a truck reaches Texas in 2 to 3 days and the Midwest in another 3 to 5 days from Laredo.

How much cash sits in inventory?

Every day in transit is inventory you have paid for and cannot sell. If you use 20,000 parts a month, 35 days of transit means about 23,300 parts are on the water at any moment. With 4 days by truck, about 2,700.

There is a second effect. With 4 to 6 weeks of transit alone, a design change, a demand spike or a bad lot gets fixed late. With days of transit, it gets fixed the following week.

How do time zones and communication affect the project?

Mexico stopped observing daylight saving time in 2022. Celaya is on UTC-6 all year: the same time as Texas in winter and one hour behind in summer. China is on UTC+8, 13 to 14 hours ahead of U.S. Central time, so each technical question often costs a day.

Physical distance matters too. A visit to approve first articles, audit a process or resolve a rejection happens without crossing the Pacific.

What about intellectual property?

In both countries, risk is reduced with contracts, not trust. What changes is the type of contract and where you can enforce it:

  • China: specialist law firms recommend an NNN agreement (non-disclosure, non-use, non-circumvention), in Chinese, under Chinese law and enforceable in Chinese courts. An NDA drafted under U.S. law is hard to enforce there.
  • Mexico: an NDA under Mexican law signed before you share drawings, plus a contract stating that the mold and the drawings belong to the customer. USMCA includes an intellectual property chapter binding all three countries.
  • In both: register your trademark and patents where you manufacture, tag the mold with an ownership plate and put in writing who may move it.

When does China still make sense?

The math does not always favor Mexico. China can win when:

  • Your HTS line is not on a Section 301 list or has an active exclusion, and the factory price gap is large.
  • The product involves heavy manual assembly or electronic components already made in the same region.
  • You sell in Asia or Europe, not in the United States.
  • The Mexican part would not meet the USMCA rule of origin and would pay 10% plus the general rate.

A hybrid model is common: build the mold at a toolmaker in Asia and run production in Mexico. You keep the tooling savings and avoid duties on every part. If you go that route, read how to transfer a mold to Mexico and how much an injection mold costs.

One risk to watch on the Mexican side: in July 2026 the U.S. declined to extend USMCA for another 16 years. The agreement remains in force, with annual reviews and a 2036 expiration unless it is renewed earlier.
