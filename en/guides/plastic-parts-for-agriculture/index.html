Plastic parts for agriculture: materials, requirements and scale

UV, chemicals, impact and seasonality: how a part that lives outdoors is designed and made.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

An agricultural part lives in the sun, around chemicals, impacts and cold, and it sells by season. That is why the usual base is HDPE or impact copolymer PP with UV protection, in black when possible. PC is ruled out near ammonia and fuels. Production is planned months ahead of the season, because demand bunches into a few weeks.

Trays, feeders, waterers, harvest crates, irrigation fittings, caps and machinery components: farms use a lot of plastic parts. All of them share four demands that an indoor part does not face.

This guide covers each one with data, which resin and which test make sense, and how to plan production so you do not miss the season.

What does sunlight do to a plastic part?

Ultraviolet radiation breaks polymer chains at the surface. The part first loses color and gloss, then turns brittle and cracks. A part that passes the impact test the day it leaves the plant can break in its second summer if it is not protected.

The best-proven protection is carbon black, which absorbs the radiation. The Plastics Pipe Institute states that black HDPE pipe, which generally contains more than 2% carbon black, resists sunlight damage indefinitely. For non-black products it recommends no more than two years of unprotected outdoor storage unless the manufacturer says otherwise.

If the part must be colored for branding or to tell models apart, it needs a UV stabilizer agreed with the resin supplier, and the expected life should be validated with a weathering test.

How many UV chamber hours equal a year in the field?

There is no universal conversion. Q-Lab, a weathering chamber maker, explains that a single conversion factor is theoretically impossible: weather changes with latitude, altitude and year.

The chamber is for comparing materials and additives against each other. To predict field life, pair it with real outdoor exposure at a site similar to where the part will be used.

Common mistake

Approving a part because it "passed 1,000 hours of UV" and promising a number of years in the field based on that. The chamber ranks materials from best to worst, but it does not give years. Ask the resin supplier for outdoor exposure data on the grade with its additive, and agree with your customer on what counts as failure: color change, loss of impact strength or cracking.

Which farm chemicals attack plastic?

Farms combine fertilizers, crop protection products, disinfectants, machinery diesel and, in livestock barns, ammonia from manure. The clearest case is polycarbonate: Covestro, its manufacturer, warns that ammonia and low-molecular-weight amines are particularly aggressive when water is present, that alkalis accelerate its hydrolysis and that it does not resist gasoline or diesel.

PP and HDPE resist non-oxidizing acids and bases, salts and detergents well, which is why they are the base for agrochemical containers and livestock parts. Even so, each agrochemical formulation is different: run an immersion test with the actual product.

Which resin takes impacts in the cold?

HDPE and impact copolymer PP. A farm part falls off a tractor, gets stacked full and takes knocks on cold mornings. PP homopolymer turns brittle below 0 °C, while impact copolymer keeps its toughness down to -40 °C. The full comparison is in the guide to PP vs. HDPE.

Nylon behaves in its own way: ambient moisture makes it tougher at room temperature, but on the Zytel 101L datasheet its impact at -30 °C falls to 3 kJ/m² when conditioned. If you are weighing other resins, use the guide to choosing a resin.

How do you plan production for a seasonal part?

Farm demand bunches up before planting or harvest. If the mold lacks the capacity to make everything within the season, you either build inventory ahead or invest in more cavities. An illustrative example shows how to run the numbers.

Assumptions: 100,000 parts a year, 70,000 of them shipped in a 10-week window. A 1-cavity mold makes 4,000 parts a week; a 2-cavity mold, 8,000.

The 2-cavity mold costs more, but it removes the early inventory and the risk of falling short if the season comes early. The full calculation is in the guide to how many cavities your mold should have, and new-project lead times in the injection molding project timeline.
