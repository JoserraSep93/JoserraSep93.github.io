Injection molding efficiency: cycle time, OEE and mold changes

What OEE is with a numeric example, where cycle time goes, the levers that lower cost per part and what to ask your molder about efficiency.

8 min read · October 22, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

An injection molding machine is efficient when it makes good parts during most of the time it is scheduled to run. The measure is OEE (availability times performance times quality). The levers that matter most are mold cooling, fast mold changes, preventive maintenance, a right-sized machine and low scrap. All of it ends up in your price per part and your delivery date.

Why does machine efficiency matter to you as a buyer?

The cost of a molded part is split between resin, machine hour and tooling. Resin costs about the same for every molder. What changes from one molder to another is how many good parts come out per machine hour. If two plants charge the same hourly rate, the one that makes 20% more good parts can quote a lower price and still come out ahead. A plant with frequent stops and rework also ships late. For the full price breakdown, see how price per part is calculated.

What is OEE and how is it calculated?

OEE stands for overall equipment effectiveness. It is the product of three percentages: availability (run time versus scheduled time), performance (actual speed versus ideal cycle speed) and quality (good parts versus total parts).

90%availability: 9 of every 10 scheduled hours the machine runs
85%performance: the real cycle is a bit slower than the ideal
95%quality: 95 of every 100 parts come out good
72.7%OEE = 0.90 × 0.85 × 0.95

Notice that three numbers that look good give an OEE of 72.7%. Vorne (OEE.com) shows another case: three factors of 90% give only 73%. The 85% "world-class" reference comes from Seiichi Nakajima's TPM work. The same source notes that many plants run closer to 60% and that the useful goal is to improve your own number, not to copy someone else's.

Example of the effect on price

Take a 4-cavity mold with a 30-second cycle. At ideal pace that is 480 parts per hour. With a machine rate of 50 USD per hour (illustrative figure):

  • At 60% OEE: 288 good parts per hour. Machine cost per part: 0.174 USD.
  • At 72.7% OEE: about 349 good parts per hour. Machine cost per part: 0.143 USD.

Raising OEE by 12.7 points cuts machine cost per part by 17%, with no change to the mold or the resin.

Where does the time go in an injection cycle?

The cycle is made of mold close, fill, pack, cooling, mold open and ejection. Cooling is almost always the longest part, and it grows fast with wall thickness. Ranges vary a lot by part and resin, so the chart is a typical split for a medium-wall part.

The practical takeaway is that taking one second off cooling pays more than taking it off any other stage.

Which levers shorten cycle time?

  • Cooling design and mold temperature control. Channels close to the part, good water flow and a stable temperature. A mold that cools evenly avoids warpage and lets you open sooner. This is a mold design decision, so it should be discussed before the mold is built.
  • Scientific molding and process window. Each phase (fill, pack, cooling) is studied separately with data instead of adjusting by feel. That finds the window where the process is stable and removes constant operator tweaks, which hurt performance and quality.
  • Right-sized machine. A machine too large for the part burns more energy and costs more per hour. One too small forces slow cycles. Tonnage and shot size should match the part and the cavity count, as covered in how many cavities to use and in the injection molding machine guide.
  • Robot or automation for part take-out. It removes operator variation in how the part drops and helps the cycle repeat the same all shift. It makes the most sense for delicate parts, parts with inserts or short cycles.
  • Preventive maintenance on mold and machine. A mold with clogged vents or dirty channels forces lower speed and stops. See the guide on mold life and maintenance.

How do you cut mold change time?

Every mold change is lost availability. SMED (single minute exchange of die) aims to bring changeover under 10 minutes in its ideal form, according to the Lean Enterprise Institute. In injection molding that extreme is rare with large molds, but going from 2 hours to 30 minutes is common with order and preparation.

Example: going from 2 hours to 30 minutes across 8 changes a month gives 12 hours of production back to the machine. If the plant runs small lots or many part numbers, this point weighs as much as cycle time.

How do scrap and energy use affect efficiency?

Scrap and regrind. Every bad part uses resin, machine time and energy. Regrind from runners and rejects can be reused in some products, but only at a controlled percentage and with customer approval, because it changes material properties. Ask what the policy is and how it is recorded.

Energy. Electric machines and servo-hydraulic machines use much less energy than conventional hydraulic ones, because the motor only works when there is movement. Manufacturers such as ENGEL report significant savings, which depend on the part and the cycle. Other simple measures are insulating the barrel with thermal blankets to lose less heat and switching off auxiliary equipment that is not in use.

Common mistake

Comparing molders only by machine hourly rate. A cheap hour with low OEE costs more per good part than a slightly higher hour with high OEE. Always compare price per part and the committed delivery date.

What should you ask a molder about efficiency?

  • Do you measure OEE by machine and by mold, and how often do you review it?
  • What cycle do you propose for my part, and where does that number come from (calculation or trial)?
  • How long does a mold change take, and how do you standardize it?
  • Do you have a preventive maintenance plan for my mold, and what is recorded?
  • How do you control scrap and what do you do with regrind?
  • How is production planned to meet my delivery date?

If the supplier answers with data and records, that is a good sign. If it answers with general statements, ask to see an example.
