Plastics for outdoor use: UV and weather resistance

Why sunlight degrades plastic, which resins and additives protect it, how weathering is tested and what to specify for agriculture and construction.

6 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Sunlight breaks plastic chains starting at the surface: first color and gloss change, then the part turns brittle and cracks. For outdoor use you pick a resin that handles weather and protect it with additives: carbon black if the part can be black, or UV stabilizers (absorbers and HALS) if it needs color. Always get the additive package and the aging test in writing, because a lab chamber ranks materials but does not promise years of life.

Why does sunlight degrade plastic?

Ultraviolet radiation starts the damage by forming free radicals in the polymer. A study on polypropylene puts it this way: PP is highly sensitive to UV radiation and oxidizing atmospheres, and degradation begins with free-radical formation. What you see is a more brittle part, a rough surface and, over time, cracks.

Heat and moisture speed it up. That is why a part that passes the impact test the day it leaves the plant can break in its second or third summer if it carries no protection. PE and PP are among the most photodegradation-prone resins; ABS yellows and loses properties in sun, and polycarbonate yellows with prolonged exposure unless protected.

Which additives protect plastic from the sun?

There are three families, and they are often combined. The figures below are typical values reported by an additives supplier; the manufacturer of each grade can give finer numbers.

2 to 3%typical carbon black level for UV protection
75:25usual HALS to UV absorber ratio in PP and HDPE
90:10usual ratio in ABS

The Plastics Pipe Institute gives a practical reference: black HDPE pipe, which generally contains more than 2% carbon black, withstands outdoor exposure with no time limit. For colored products it advises no more than two years of unprotected outdoor storage unless the manufacturer recommends otherwise. It is a piping criterion, but it shows the gap between a black part and a colored one.

It depends on what else the part must withstand. The table gives the general picture; confirm the final choice against the grade's datasheet.

If you are torn between PP and HDPE for a field product, the guide on PP vs. HDPE compares them. The agricultural case, with chemicals and cold, is in the guide on plastic parts for agriculture.

How is aging tested?

With accelerated weathering chambers and, when the project justifies it, outdoor exposure. The most used standards are ASTM G154 (fluorescent UV lamps, QUV type) and ASTM G155 (xenon arc); on the ISO side, the 4892 series covers both methods for plastics.

Chamber hours do not convert to field years with a single factor. Q-Lab, which makes these instruments, explains that a universal factor is theoretically impossible: degradation can differ up to 2 to 1 from one year to the next at the same site, and winter exposure can be seven times less severe than summer. The chamber ranks materials from best to worst; it does not promise a service life.

What if the part must be colored?

Carbon black rules out every other color, so a red, blue or white part depends on UV stabilizers and on pigments that withstand sun. According to Safic-Alcan, white with rutile TiO₂ helps because it scatters UV, and the anatase type speeds up degradation. Intensely colored organic pigments tend to fade sooner than inorganic ones, so ask the masterbatch supplier for the lightfastness data of each color.

The practical consequence: a colored outdoor part costs more and takes more care to validate than a black one. If color is not part of the function, black is the simplest and most proven option.

Example: how much masterbatch a black part needs

Illustrative example. A 500 g PP part must carry 2% carbon black. A black masterbatch with 40% carbon black is used (assumed concentration; the supplier gives the actual one for its product).

With the dosage set, the remaining job is making sure it mixes evenly: uneven distribution leaves areas of the part with less protection. Color and masterbatch are covered in the guide on color in plastic parts.

Common mistake

Approving a part because it "passed 1,000 hours of UV" and writing a multi-year life into the quote. Without outdoor exposure data for the exact grade and without defining what counts as failure (color change, loss of impact or a crack), the promise has no basis.

What should you specify for agriculture and construction?

  • Resin and exact grade, with its datasheet and the declared UV stabilization system.
  • Color: black if the function allows it. If it must be colored, state that pigment and stabilizer are agreed with the supplier.
  • Aging test: standard (G154, G155 or ISO 4892), hours and failure criterion in writing.
  • Wall thickness: in thin walls the protection runs out sooner. See the injection molding design rules.
  • Freeze the material: changing resin brand or color changes the protection, so it is approved in writing.

What should you ask the molder before quoting?

  • Which resin grade do they propose, and which UV stabilization system does its datasheet declare?
  • Who doses the masterbatch, and how is the agreed percentage controlled on every lot?
  • Is in-house regrind used? A high share of reprocessed material can reduce protection, so a maximum is set in writing.
  • Which aging test will be run, at which lab and with what pass criterion?
