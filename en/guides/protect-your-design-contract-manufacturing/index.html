How to protect your design when outsourcing: NDAs, mold ownership and IP

What to sign before sending drawings, how to make mold ownership clear and how risks differ by country.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Sign a non-disclosure agreement before you send any drawing, put in writing that you own the mold and on what terms it is loaned to the molder, and register your patents and trademarks in every country where you manufacture or sell. The right contract, and where it can be enforced, depend on the supplier's country. This guide explains concepts; for your contract, talk to a lawyer.

What should I sign before sending drawings to a contract manufacturer?

A non-disclosure agreement (NDA). It is the first barrier, and it should be signed before you share the 3D file, the drawing or the spec sheet. Once the information is out, an agreement signed later does not cover what was already shared.

There is an added legal reason. In Mexico, the Federal Law for the Protection of Industrial Property (Article 163) treats information as a trade secret only if its owner adopted "sufficient means or systems to preserve its confidentiality." In the U.S., the Defend Trade Secrets Act requires "reasonable measures" to the same effect. A signed agreement is part of that evidence.

What should a good NDA include?

Harris Sliwoski, a law firm focused on China, stresses one point: unauthorized use of your information is usually a bigger commercial threat than its disclosure. An NDA that only bars disclosure leaves the most dangerous door open.

Who owns the mold, and how do you put it in writing?

Paying for the mold is not enough to make ownership clear. The contract or purchase order should state that the mold, its drawings and its spare parts belong to the customer, and what happens to it if the relationship ends. A simple practice is to fit the mold with a tag showing the owner's name and an ID number.

Harris Sliwoski makes another point: an NDA protects the information; it does not establish who owns the mold. That belongs in a separate manufacturing or tooling agreement.

What is a comodato agreement, and how does it apply to a mold?

In Mexico, a comodato is a contract in which one party lends the use of an asset free of charge and the other agrees to return it (Federal Civil Code, Article 2497). Applied to a mold: you own it and lend it to the molder only to make your parts.

Art. 2500the borrower cannot let a third party use the asset without the owner's permission
Art. 2509the borrower cannot hold the asset on the grounds that the owner owes it money
Art. 2511if no term was set, the owner can ask for it back at any time

These articles are from Mexico's Federal Civil Code. Each Mexican state has its own civil code with similar rules, and which one applies depends on the contract. Your lawyer should confirm which one governs and what to add: mold insurance, who pays for maintenance and how quickly it must be returned.

Can a molder keep my mold?

It depends on the country and the contract. Several U.S. states have a molder's lien: a law that lets the molder hold the mold if the customer does not pay. In Florida, for example, after written notice and 60 days without payment, the molder may sell the mold at public auction, unless there is a good-faith dispute over quality or the amount owed.

The practical way to avoid it is the same anywhere: stay current on payments, document mold ownership and agree on a return procedure. If you plan to move a mold between plants, see how to transfer a mold to a plant in Mexico.

Common mistake

Using the same English-language NDA under U.S. law for a supplier in any country. If the supplier and its assets are abroad, a U.S. court judgment has to be recognized there, which adds cost and time. The agreement must be enforceable where the supplier is.

How does IP risk differ by country?

No country brings the risk to zero; it is reduced with contracts you can actually enforce. The full comparison of cost, time and risk is in manufacturing in Mexico or China.

Who owns the improvements the molder suggests?

During a design review, molders often suggest changes: a radius, a wall thickness, a rib or a gate adjustment. If the contract is silent, ownership of that improvement can be unclear. The usual approach is to agree that improvements made to your part or your mold belong to the customer, while the molder keeps its general process know-how.

The same goes for mold drawings: ask that the toolmaker deliver the mold design files along with the mold. Without them, repairing or replicating a mold component at another shop costs more and takes longer.

Do my patents and trademarks protect me in another country?

Only if they are registered there. WIPO puts it this way: patents, trademarks and industrial designs are territorial rights and only have legal force in the country or region where they were granted. If you manufacture in Mexico and sell in the U.S., consider registering in both.
