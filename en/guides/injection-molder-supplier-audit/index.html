How to audit a plastic injection molding supplier at their plant

What to check during a plant visit: machines, molds, materials, quality, traceability and housekeeping, with a list of questions per area.

6 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A plant visit lets you confirm with your own eyes what the supplier told you on paper. Request the documents beforehand, walk the plant in the order the material moves (warehouse, drying, press, mold, inspection, packing) and bring a list of questions per area, with the evidence you want to see for each. Decide in advance what result makes you approve, approve with conditions or drop the supplier. This guide is the practical checklist for that day.

If you are still building your shortlist, start with how to choose an injection molder, which covers the earlier filters and how to compare quotes. Here we assume you already have one or two candidates and are about to visit them.

What should I request before the visit?

The visit pays off more when you have already read the documents and only need to verify what they say. Ask for them a few days ahead:

  • Current ISO certificate and internal audit schedule.
  • Press list with tonnage and the preventive maintenance plan.
  • Examples of nonconformances and their corrective actions.
  • Material certificates, lot labels and dryer logs.
  • Calibration records and inspection reports for parts similar to yours.
  • Mold list, mold condition and maintenance records.
  • Packaging specification and shipping records.

What red flags can I spot at a glance?

  • Unlabeled or open resin: if nobody knows what it is or which lot it came from, traceability breaks at the start.
  • Molds stacked without ID or with rust: suggests reactive maintenance.
  • Rejected parts mixed with good ones: nonconforming product is not segregated.
  • Instruments with no calibration sticker: you cannot trust their readings.
  • Process settings taped to the press by hand: anyone can change them without control.
  • Evasive answers when you ask for a record: if they cannot show one on site, it usually does not exist.

How do I check traceability during the visit?

Pick a lot that was already produced and ask them to follow it backward: from shipment to the production order, then to the press and shift, and finally to the resin lot and its drying record. If the supplier does it in minutes from their system, traceability exists. If they ask for a day to put it together, it is manual and fragile. Also ask to see how regrind and process changes are recorded.

What do I do with an ISO certificate during the visit?

An ISO 9001:2015 certificate indicates an audited quality management system exists, and ISO 14001:2015 indicates an environmental one. Neither guarantees the quality of a specific part. What helps is seeing how it is applied on the floor: whether the procedure the manual cites matches what the operator does at the press. Verify the certificate with the issuing body and check its scope and validity.

Set the criteria before you walk in

Write down what counts as a pass before the visit: lot traceability on every shipment, first-article approval before production, documented mold maintenance. Without criteria set in advance, the audit turns into a conversation about impressions. And confirm each finding with your host before leaving, because arguing about it later by email is harder.

How do I close the visit and what comes next?

Summarize by area what was good, what is conditional and what is missing. Send the report in writing a few days later and ask for an action plan with an owner and a date for each finding. If you are transferring a mold or launching a new part, the next step is the first sample with a dimensional report, which we explain in the guide on PPAP and first article inspection. To understand what a supplier can measure in house and what goes to an outside lab, see the guide on quality control for plastic parts.
