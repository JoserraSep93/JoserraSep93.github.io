Glass-filled plastics: when to use them and what changes in the part

How much stiffness increases, what happens to warpage and mold wear, and when it is not worth it.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

With 30% glass fiber, the stiffness of PP or nylon 66 rises 3 to 5 times, strength 2 to 3 times, and the temperature the part can carry load at climbs more than 100 °C. In exchange, the part gets less ductile, warps more because it shrinks differently along and across the flow, weighs 20 to 26% more and wears the mold, which should be built in hardened steel.

Glass fiber is the most common way to turn a general-purpose resin into a structural material. It shows up in brackets, load-bearing housings, parts near motors and components that used to be metal.

This guide uses datasheets to show how much a part changes going from 0 to 30% glass, what problems come with it and when another solution makes more sense.

What is a glass-filled plastic?

It is a resin that the compounder blends with short glass fibers before pelletizing. It is named by base resin and weight percentage: PP-GF30 or PA66-GF30 means 30% glass fiber. Protolabs reports that commercial loadings typically run from 13 to 45%.

The fibers work like rebar in concrete: they carry part of the stress and resist deformation. That is why stiffness, strength and load-bearing temperature all go up.

How much do stiffness and strength increase with 30% glass?

We compare two pairs of datasheets from the same manufacturers: an unfilled PP homopolymer against a 30% glass PP, and an unfilled nylon 66 against a 30% glass nylon 66.

× 4.7PP stiffness with 30% glass (1,290 to 6,000 MPa)
× 3.2nylon 66 stiffness with 30% glass (3,100 to 10,000 MPa)
× 2.4 to 2.7tensile strength in both cases

In practice, a glass-filled part can use thinner walls for the same stiffness, or carry more load with the same geometry. That is how a metal bracket gets redesigned in plastic.

How much temperature does the part gain?

This is the biggest change. Under a 1.8 MPa load, unfilled PP deforms at 49 °C while PP-GF30 holds to 160 °C. Nylon 66 goes from 70 °C to 253 °C, close to its 262 °C melting point.

Why do glass-filled parts warp more?

Because the fibers line up with the plastic's flow as it fills the mold, and the part shrinks differently depending on direction. Protolabs puts it this way: glass fibers restrict shrink in a direction-dependent way, which creates nonlinear shrink and adds the internal stress that increases warp risk.

Example with a 200 mm (7.9 in) plate. Unfilled, it shrinks 2.8 mm along and 2.8 mm across: it gets smaller but stays flat. With 30% glass it shrinks 0.6 mm along and 2.0 mm across. That 1.4 mm difference between directions is released as warp.

It is controlled at the design stage: uniform walls, well-oriented ribs and a gate location chosen so the fibers align with the main load. Basic geometry rules are in the guide to injection molding design rules, and the design for manufacturing review is where it gets caught.

Common mistake

Switching to a glass-filled grade in a mold built for the unfilled resin to "make the part stronger." Shrinkage goes from an even 1.4% to 0.3 and 1.0% depending on direction, so dimensions fall out of tolerance and the part twists. The mold steel may not be ready for the abrasion either. Changing material in an existing mold calls for a dimensional and steel review.

How much does glass fiber wear the mold?

Considerably more than an unfilled resin. Böhler, a mold steel producer, notes that fiber-reinforced plastics are much more abrasive than conventional plastics and can cause premature mold wear. Gates, thin edges and areas where the flow hits steel head-on wear first.

That is why the resin should be settled before the mold is ordered. How much steel moves the price is covered in the guide to injection mold cost, and the service schedule in the one on mold life and maintenance.

What does a part give up with glass fiber?

25% → 3%elongation at break of dry nylon 66 when 30% glass is added: the part stretches far less before it breaksDuPont, Zytel 101L and 70G30HSLR datasheets
  • Ductility: snap fits and living hinges need to flex without breaking. At 3% elongation they are very limited. Protolabs sums it up: with strength comes brittleness.
  • Weight: density rises 26% in PP (0.90 to 1.13) and 20% in nylon (1.14 to 1.37). Since resin is bought by the pound, each part uses more resin for the same volume.
  • Appearance: fibers can show on the surface and leave visible flow lines. Cosmetic parts should be sampled first.

When is glass fiber not worth it?

  • When the part takes repeated impacts and deflection instead of a steady load. Protolabs recommends glass for parts in a stable environment that are mainly supporting weight.
  • When the problem is geometry: a well-placed rib can add the missing stiffness without changing resin.
  • When volume is low and a hardened steel mold will not pay for itself.
  • When the part has snap fits or hinges that must flex.
