Injection molding clamping force: what it is and how to calculate it

What clamping force is, why it prevents flash and mold damage, the formula with resin factors, a step-by-step example and why extra tonnage is a waste.

6 min read · October 22, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Clamp tonnage is the force the press uses to hold the mold shut while plastic is injected. You estimate it from the projected area of the part, the number of cavities and a factor that depends on the resin. Too little and parts come out with flash. Too much and you pay for a bigger press by the hour. You can run the numbers in minutes with our clamp tonnage calculator.

What is the tonnage of an injection molding machine?

It is the clamping force: what the clamp unit can hold without letting the mold open. It is measured in tons. A 300 t press can keep a mold shut when the pressure inside pushes with less than that 300 t.

Plastic enters under pressure and tries to push the two mold halves apart. The press has to beat that force on every cycle. To see where tonnage fits in the rest of the press, read the injection molding machine guide.

Why does it matter to get it right?

  • Too little tonnage: the mold opens a few microns, plastic seeps along the parting line and the part comes out with flash. That extra work is paid for in trimming or scrap.
  • Too little tonnage, again: the mold suffers. Repeated flash damages the parting line, and that repair is expensive.
  • Too much tonnage: the mold gets crushed, vents close up and wear speeds up. The press is also bigger, so its hourly rate is higher.

What is the formula to calculate tonnage?

The rule of thumb is simple: total projected area times the resin factor. Projected area is the shadow of the part seen from the direction the mold opens. You add up all cavities and the runners.

Tonnage = total projected area × resin factor × adjustments × margin

The calculator uses exactly the same method, so what you read here and what the tool returns agree.

Which factor do I use for each resin?

These are typical industry values, used as a rule of thumb. An easy-flow resin needs less force than a viscous one. Your resin supplier can give a more precise figure for the specific grade.

What makes the tonnage go up?

  • Thin walls: you have to inject faster and at higher pressure to reach the end of the fill. That is why we multiply by 1.25 or 1.5.
  • Long flow-length-to-wall ratio: the farther the plastic travels compared with the wall thickness, the more pressure it takes.
  • Runner area: the runner also sees pressure. In a cold runner it adds about 10%.
  • Glass fiber: filled resin is more viscous and needs about 20% more. We cover it in the guide to glass-filled plastic.
  • High-viscosity resins: PC, PA66 and similar grades need more force than PP or PE.

Example: a PP part with 4 cavities

A polypropylene part with normal walls, 200 cm² of projected area and a 4-cavity mold.

The range is about 186 to 340 t. To be safe, look for a press that covers the high value, so 340 t or more.

Common mistake

Calculating with the area of one part and forgetting cavities and runners. With 4 cavities, the error is fourfold. Another frequent mistake is treating the low end of the range as enough: flash shows up exactly when the press runs at its limit.

Why is buying extra tonnage a bad idea?

A 10% margin protects you. A 100% margin gets expensive. A bigger press has a higher machine rate, uses more energy and the mold must fit between its tie bars. That cost is spread over the parts and raises the price of each one.

Excess clamp force also crushes vents and wears the parting line of the mold. The goal is the smallest press that covers the high value with a reasonable margin.

How does tonnage connect with machine choice and cost per part?

Tonnage decides which presses can run your mold. From there come the machine rate, the number of cavities you can use and the cost per part. More cavities mean more area and a bigger press: we explain it in the guide on how many cavities to use. To estimate the final price, see how the price per part is calculated.

Shot capacity, mold size and press opening also count. Tonnage is the first filter, not the only one.
