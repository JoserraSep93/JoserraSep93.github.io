Prototyping before the mold: 3D printing, machining and prototype tooling

What each type of prototype validates, how close it is to the molded part and how to choose based on what you need to test.

7 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Choose the prototype based on what you need to test. 3D printing works for shape and fit in the first versions. CNC machining works for functional tests with engineering-grade material. A prototype mold, usually aluminum, gives you injection molded parts in the final resin and comes closest to production. The closer you get to the final part, the more it costs and the longer it takes. So move up a step only when the previous one can no longer answer your question.

What is a prototype before the mold for?

The production mold is the biggest investment in the project and fixing it is expensive. A prototype lets you be wrong early, when changing the design costs a file and not a steel re-machining. Formlabs puts it as iterating on the design and testing the end-use material before committing to hard tooling.

Each prototype answers different questions. Before choosing, write down what you want to check: whether the part looks right, whether it fits the others, whether it holds a load or whether the injection process can make it. To see what the final mold costs, read the guide on injection mold cost.

What are the options, and what does each one validate?

One important point: a printed or machined part does not reveal whether the design can be molded. A 3D print can have wall thicknesses or undercuts that a mold cannot produce. Only a molded part, even from a prototype mold, shows you shrinkage, weld lines and fill. We cover how to anticipate that in the guide on mold flow simulation.

When do I use each one?

  • 3D printing: first versions, form models, fit checks and quantities of one to a few parts. It is the fastest and cheapest per part.
  • CNC machining: when you need a load, temperature or fit test with material close to the final one, in small quantities. It has limits with some undercuts and deep ribs.
  • Aluminum prototype mold: when you need dozens or hundreds of parts in the production resin, for customer trials, validation or certification. Protolabs offers single-cavity prototype molds aimed at fewer than 2,000 parts, which is a useful industry reference.

Not every project needs all five steps. A simple part with a proven design can go straight to the mold. A part with a complex assembly or demanding mechanical requirements benefits from walking through them. This changes the schedule: see the guide on the injection molding project timeline.

How much does each option cost?

Prices vary by provider, size and material, so there is no single figure. As an industry reference, Formlabs gives a hypothetical example: a 3D printed mold for about 100 parts at about $100, an aluminum mold for about 5,000 parts at about $3,000 and a steel mold for 100,000 parts at about $20,000. Those are illustrative values from an article, not quotes, and production molds for industrial parts often cost considerably more.

Common mistake

Approving a design with printed parts and finding out at the production mold that the final material behaves differently. Shrinkage, brittleness and finish change with the molded resin. If the part is critical, validate at least once with the final resin.

What happens with the design you already tested?

Each prototype should leave written changes: what was tested, what failed and what was modified. Those notes feed into the injection molding design rules and reduce adjustment rounds on the production mold.
