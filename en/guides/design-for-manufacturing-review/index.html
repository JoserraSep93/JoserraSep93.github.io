What a design for manufacturing review is and what it prevents

The defects prevented at the design stage, what they cost to fix later and what we check on every part.

8 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

A design for manufacturing (DFM) review checks the 3D model before any steel is cut, to catch what will cause defects: uneven walls, missing draft, thick ribs, a poorly placed gate. Fixing it in CAD costs engineering hours; in the mold, steel and weeks; in production, you pay for it on every part.

Almost every defect in a molded part has a cause that was already in the drawing. The process can compensate a little, but every compensation adds cycle time or scrap. This guide covers what gets checked, which defects it prevents and what skipping it costs.

What is a design for manufacturing review?

It is a check of the part against the rules of injection molding: wall thickness, draft angles, ribs, screw bosses, radii, gate location, resin and tolerances. The output is a list of recommended changes, each with its reason, before the mold design is released.

The review does not change what the part does. It changes how the part is drawn so the mold fills it completely, cools it evenly and ejects it without damage.

What does it cost to fix a defect in design, in the mold or in production?

In design, a fix means moving a face in CAD: engineering hours. In the mold, it means machining or welding steel and sampling again. In production, the fix lives in every cycle: extra seconds of cooling, rejected parts, 100% sorting or, at worst, a new insert.

Design steel-safe

Removing metal from a mold is relatively easy; adding it can be difficult or impossible. On the part, that means thickening a wall, adding a rib or shrinking a hole can be done later. Thinning a wall, removing a rib or enlarging a hole means remaking the insert. When you are unsure about a dimension, put it on the side that leaves steel in the mold.

Which design rules prevent those defects?

Three rules solve most of the problems in the table, and a fourth number shows why thinner walls pay off. They are starting points: the resin and the geometry may call for adjustments.

40 to 60%rib thickness relative to the wall it sits on
0.5° min.draft on vertical faces
1° per inchof draft for every inch of cavity depth
-36%cooling time when the wall is 20% thinner (see below)

Another key rule is to keep wall thickness even and within the range the resin accepts. A wall outside that range fills poorly when it is too thin and sinks or warps when it is too thick.

Why does wall thickness drive the cost per part?

Because cooling takes most of the cycle. RJG, a training and process control company for injection molding, estimates that about 80% of the molding cycle is spent cooling the part. In its worked example, a 43-second cycle spends 85% of the time cooling.

Thickness enters the cooling equation squared. If the wall grows 50%, cooling time grows 125%. If the wall doubles, cooling time goes up four times.

A worked example using RJG's cycle: cut the wall by 20% and cooling drops 36% (0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64). The 36.6 seconds of cooling become 23.4, and the cycle goes from 43 to about 30 seconds. Per cavity, output rises from 84 to 121 parts per hour, 44% more. At 200,000 parts a year, that is about 730 fewer machine hours. The math assumes the rest of the cycle stays the same and that ribs keep the part as strong as before.

How does the resin affect warp?

Each resin shrinks differently as it cools. The mold is cut oversize to make up for nominal shrinkage; the trouble starts when one area of the part shrinks differently from another.

A 12-inch PP part shrinks between 0.12 and 0.36 in. Semi-crystalline resins such as PP and HDPE shrink more and are more prone to warp than ABS or PC. In glass-filled compounds, shrinkage changes with flow direction, so the gate location decides which way the part can bow. To choose between the two most common resins, see polypropylene vs. HDPE.

When should you ask for a DFM review?

With the first 3D model, before the design is frozen and before the final mold quote. It is the only stage where a change costs hours. If the mold already exists, as in a mold transfer to Mexico, the review covers the part and the mold together to see what can still be adjusted in steel.

To request one, send the 3D model and drawing with the information listed in what you need to get a part quoted.

How SDH does it

The review is done by the group's R&D department, which holds more than 50 patents, and comes with the quote where it applies.
