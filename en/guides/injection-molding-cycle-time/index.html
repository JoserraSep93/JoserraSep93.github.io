Injection molding cycle time: what drives it and how it affects price

The stages of the cycle, why cooling dominates, how to estimate it from wall thickness and an example of how much it changes price per part.

7 min read · November 11, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Cycle time is how long the press takes to make one shot, from start to finish. Cooling is usually the longest stage, from half to more than 80% of the cycle depending on the part, and it grows with the square of wall thickness: double the wall and cooling takes four times as long. Because the press is billed by the hour, every second of cycle goes straight into the price per part. This guide does not repeat OEE or mold changeovers; those are covered in the guide on injection molding efficiency.

Why does cooling dominate?

The plastic enters at around 200 °C or more and has to drop to a temperature where the part can be ejected without deforming. Heat leaves through the mold walls, which are cooled by water channels. The thicker the wall, the farther the heat has to travel from the center.

BASF sums it up this way: the heat to remove falls linearly as the wall gets thinner, but the cooling time falls quadratically. Half the thickness means a quarter of the time, which is why wall thickness is the most influential factor.

t ∝ s²cooling time grows with the square of wall thickness
50 to 85%of the cycle is usually cooling, depending on thickness and geometry
10 to 30%possible deviation between the theoretical formula and a specific part

How do you estimate cooling from wall thickness?

The full formula uses the resin's thermal diffusivity and the melt, mold wall and ejection temperatures. For a first idea, the square rule is enough: start from a known point and scale by (new thickness / base thickness)².

Using a reference point from a technical calculator, a polypropylene part with a 2.5 mm wall needs about 8 s of cooling; with 5 mm, about 32 s. We take that point plus a fixed 6 s for closing, filling, opening and ejection.

How much does the price per part change?

Press time is spread across the parts that come out in that hour. If the cycle gets longer, fewer parts come out and each one carries more machine cost. With an illustrative rate of USD 60 per press hour (not an SDH rate or anyone else's), and a 4-cavity mold:

Thinning the wall from 2.5 to 2.0 mm cuts the cycle by about 21% and machine cost per part by the same proportion. Going from 2.5 to 5 mm multiplies it by 2.7. To see what else goes into the price, read how price per part is calculated.

Common mistake

Asking for a thicker wall "for safety" without checking its cost in cycle time. If the part holds up at 2.5 mm, 5 mm rarely makes it twice as strong, but it does make it four times slower to cool. A well-designed rib often gives stiffness with less material. Wall and rib rules are in the injection molding design rules.

What else changes cooling time?

The resin matters too. Thermal diffusivity tells you how fast heat moves through the material: the lower the value, the slower the cooling.

Ejection temperature matters as well. As a rule of thumb, for amorphous materials it is close to the glass transition temperature; for semicrystalline ones, 10 to 20 °C below the HDT. Ejecting too early causes warpage and out-of-tolerance dimensions. Waiting longer lengthens the cycle for no gain.

How can the cycle be shortened?

Hot runners and their pros and cons are explained in the guide on hot runner vs. cold runner. And the number of cavities trades off between cycle and tonnage; see how many cavities to run.
