Aluminum vs. steel molds: which one fits your project

Cost, tool life, lead time and finish for each option, and when a bridge tool avoids a bad launch.

7 min read · October 20, 2026 · Reviewed by SDH Plastics engineering

The short answer

Aluminum fits prototypes, launches and runs of thousands to tens of thousands of parts: it costs less and is built in weeks. Steel fits programs above 100,000 parts, abrasive resins and tight tolerances held for years. If you need parts soon and volume later, an aluminum bridge tool plus a steel production tool usually beats forcing one mold to do both jobs.

What is the difference between an aluminum mold and a steel mold?

Both make the same part. What changes is how long they last, what they cost and how fast they are built. Mold-grade aluminum (alloys such as 7075 or QC-10) is soft: Fictiv says it machines 3 to 10 times faster than steel, so the tool arrives sooner and costs less.

Steel (pre-hardened P20, hardened H13 or S136-type stainless) is hard. It withstands injection pressure, resin abrasion and clamp impact for hundreds of thousands or millions of cycles.

How much does an aluminum mold cost compared with a steel mold?

Protolabs lists aluminum molds from about $1,500 for simple parts. Prototek gives $1,000 to $6,000 for aluminum versus $25,000 to over $100,000 for steel. Xcentric frames it as a ratio: aluminum costs one quarter to one half of a steel tool for the same part.

The metal itself is a small share of the price. Machining and fitting hours drive it. That is why a large aluminum part with several slides can cost more than a simple steel cap tool. The full breakdown is in how much an injection mold costs.

How many parts can an aluminum mold make?

The most quoted figure is 10,000 parts, which Protolabs uses as a rule of thumb while noting that tools often exceed it. With good design, non-abrasive resin and maintenance, several shops report 50,000 to more than 100,000 parts.

Does an aluminum mold run faster?

For many parts, yes. Aluminum conducts heat 3 to 5 times better than mold steel (about 130 to 160 W/m·K versus 30 to 40 W/m·K for P20, per RapidDirect and steel data sheets). Cooling is the largest share of the cycle, so the part ejects sooner.

Prototek reports cycles up to 25% shorter and Xcentric cites studies showing 25 to 40% less. These come from aluminum tooling suppliers, so read them as a best case. Thin-wall parts see a smaller gain.

Example: mold cost per part by program volume

An aluminum mold is cheap, but if the program outlives it you need another one, and every new tool needs another tryout and sample approval. This example spreads the mold cost across the parts.

Assumptions (illustrative, not a quote): unfilled PP part, 1 cavity. Aluminum: $8,000 and 50,000-cycle life. P20 steel: $20,000 and 300,000 cycles. H13 steel: $32,000 and 1 million cycles.

The takeaway: at 10,000 parts aluminum wins easily. At 100,000 it still competes if you accept one tool replacement. At a million, hardened steel wins on cost and stability. If your volume is low, also see the minimum volume for injection molding.

What is a bridge tool and when does it make sense?

A bridge tool is a fast mold, usually aluminum, that makes parts while the steel production tool is being built. RapidDirect describes it as the step between prototyping and mass production, typically for 2,000 to 5,000 beta units.

A bridge tool is an added investment, but it lowers the risk of hardening steel around a design that is still moving. The full launch sequence is in the injection molding project timeline.

Common mistake

Specifying aluminum for a glass-filled resin. Glass fiber scratches and erodes aluminum, so the part loses dimensions and finish well before the expected life. Protolabs and Prototek recommend steel for abrasive or corrosive resins. More in glass-filled plastics.

How do I choose between aluminum and steel for my part?

  • Total program volume: under a few tens of thousands of parts, aluminum; above 100,000, steel.
  • Resin: glass, mineral fill or corrosive additives call for hardened steel or stainless.
  • Urgency: if you need parts in weeks, aluminum or a bridge tool.
  • Design maturity: if it will still change, aluminum; changes cost less.
  • Tolerances and finish over years: steel.
